The brain and balance

Keywords

balance control, sensory integration, neural oscillations, cognitive set shifting, postural control

Learning objectives

  1. Identify the four major sensory systems that contribute to balance — vision, vestibular, cutaneous, and proprioception — and explain why no single system provides sufficient information on its own.
  2. Describe how the brain’s hierarchical control of balance (spinal cord, brainstem, cortex) shifts from automatic to voluntary processing as task difficulty increases or as aging and neurological disease progress.
  3. Explain why cognitive flexibility and motor adaptability are linked, and how both relate to fall risk in older adults and people with neurological conditions such as Parkinson’s disease.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Lena Ting

Overview

Why Balance Control Matters

Balance control is foundational to virtually all voluntary movement. Babies must achieve standing before they can walk, and loss of balance ability can cascade into loss of independence, mobility, and quality of life. In adults over 65, falls are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality — consequences that include injury, loss of independence, and a negative spiral in overall health. Balance impairment is a feature of an exceptionally broad range of conditions spanning musculoskeletal, neurological, and psychiatric disorders, precisely because balance depends on the coordinated function of nearly every part of the nervous system: sensory systems, motor systems, and cognitive systems alike. Even medications — particularly those that cause drowsiness in hospitalized older adults — can substantially elevate fall risk.

Because balance is impaired across so many conditions and populations, it is a tractable entry point for studying fundamental principles of motor control. Balance control lends itself to controlled laboratory study in ways that locomotion does not, while sharing the same underlying neural principles — the mechanisms responsible for corrective balance responses during standing have been found to generalize to corrective torques during perturbed walking as well.

Balance as an Automatic Behavior

Balance control is normally automatic, much like breathing. Under ordinary conditions it is governed by subcortical circuits — the spinal cord and brainstem — without requiring conscious attention. This is evident in everyday experience: a young healthy person can walk and send text messages simultaneously, with neither task demanding full conscious attention. However, in more challenging environments, during difficult tasks, or as a consequence of aging or disease, control of balance shifts toward higher cortical circuits where processing is more deliberate and voluntary.

This shift is a natural and healthy form of flexibility. The problem arises when people must rely on cortical control for activities that should remain automatic — leaving fewer cognitive resources for other simultaneous demands. This is the mechanism behind the common clinical observation that people with fall risk slow down when asked to walk and perform a cognitive task at the same time. It also explains why balance and cognitive health are intertwined: as balance consumes more cortical resources, the margin for other mental activity narrows.

The Sensory Foundations of Balance

Maintaining balance requires answering three distinct questions simultaneously: which way is up (postural orientation), am I falling (equilibrium), and what is my body configuration (posture)? No single sensory system answers all three, which means the nervous system must continuously integrate signals from multiple sources.

Vision can detect relative motion between the observer and the environment, and when a reliable visual reference such as a horizon or vertical wall is present, it can help establish which direction is up. However, vision cannot distinguish whether the observer is moving or the environment is moving — a moving visual surround can trigger a balance response even when the body is stationary, because the nervous system interprets the visual motion as self-motion. Vision also provides no direct information about body configuration unless a person is actively looking at their own limbs.

The vestibular system, located in the inner ear, comprises fluid-filled canals oriented in three planes that detect rotational head motion, and otolith organs that detect linear acceleration and the pull of gravity. The otolith organs function similarly to a small stone resting on a bed of hair cells: wherever gravity or acceleration pushes the stone, the underlying cells fire. This system can indicate which way is up, but tilting the head and accelerating sideways produce the same otolith signal — the two are ambiguous. The vestibular system also encodes head motion, not whole-body configuration, so it cannot directly describe limb posture.

Cutaneous receptors in the skin — especially the pressure-sensitive receptors in the soles of the feet — are critical for balance. The distribution of pressure across the foot surface acts as a continuous map of where ground reaction forces are applied, providing information about the direction of body lean. Cooling the feet reduces this sensation and measurably impairs the ability to stand steadily.

Proprioceptors are particularly important for characterizing body configuration. Muscle spindles are modified muscle fibers that act as sensory organs, firing when their parent muscle is stretched. Golgi tendon organs, located at the muscle-tendon junction, respond to tendon tension. Together these receptors give rise to kinesthesia — the sense of where each body segment is relative to the others. Muscle spindles are also the basis of the familiar tendon-tap reflex: striking the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps, activating muscle spindle afferents that travel to the spinal cord and cause the same muscle to contract reflexively — a simple example of spinal cord feedback control.

Because none of these systems alone can answer all three balance questions, the nervous system must integrate all of them continuously. Research using blindfolded participants on a tilting platform illustrates this integration: at small tilt amplitudes, participants maintain relatively fixed ankle angles and lean with the platform — a proprioceptive strategy for maintaining joint configuration. As tilt amplitude grows, a vestibular strategy takes over and the head remains relatively stable with respect to gravity even as the platform rotates underneath. People with vestibular loss cannot make this transition and passively tilt with the platform regardless of amplitude.

The ability to shift dynamically between sensory inputs — weighting them according to their reliability in the current context — is a hallmark of a healthy nervous system. When one modality is compromised (as in diabetic neuropathy affecting foot sensation), other senses can partially compensate. Sensory conflict between channels — for example, proprioception indicating one tilt while a moving visual scene suggests another — can produce disorientation or motion sickness. A practical remedy for this conflict, such as the advice to look at the horizon when feeling nauseated on a boat, works by providing a reliable gravitational reference that overrides the conflicting indoor signals.

Perceptual Thresholds and Whole-Body Motion Perception

Effective balance correction requires knowing which direction the body is falling before the right muscle response can be selected. Perceptual thresholds for whole-body motion direction can be measured using a psychophysical paradigm: standing participants are perturbed directly backward on a movable platform, allowed to restabilize over approximately five seconds, and then perturbed again at an angular offset from the backward direction. The participant judges whether the two perturbations felt the same or different. The perceptual threshold — the angular difference at which correct discrimination occurs 50% of the time — is estimated from the resulting psychometric curve.

Two properties of this psychometric curve carry distinct information. The threshold (also called acuity) represents the minimum detectable angular difference. The slope of the curve around the threshold represents perceptual sensitivity — how consistently a person distinguishes perturbations once they are beyond that threshold. A steep slope indicates high certainty in perceptual judgments near the boundary; a shallow slope indicates variability and uncertainty even for clearly different angles.

Research findings show thresholds of approximately 9.5 degrees on average in young adults. Thresholds are elevated in older adults and elevated further in people with Parkinson’s disease and stroke; in some stroke patients, thresholds from ongoing research have been observed approaching approximately 50 degrees — meaning a perturbation aimed nearly perpendicular to backward still felt identical to one directly backward.

In people with Parkinson’s disease (but not in healthy older adults), perceptual threshold correlates significantly with clinical balance scores on the Mini Balance Evaluation Systems Test (Mini-BESTest; maximum score 28). This correlation suggests that some balance deficits in Parkinson’s disease may reflect impaired perception of falling direction rather than impaired muscle force generation. Sensory deficits in Parkinson’s disease appear to span multiple modalities, including proprioceptive and visual processing, rather than being limited to a single channel.

Neural Hierarchies: Spinal Cord, Brainstem, and Cortex

Balance responses arise from a hierarchy of neural circuits operating at different time delays and serving different functional roles. Three levels are most relevant.

At the spinal cord level, responses begin at approximately 50 ms after a perturbation onset. This circuitry drives joint-level co-contraction and stretch reflexes that stiffen joints and maintain local positions. Because spinal circuits operate primarily on proprioceptive signals from individual joints, this level functions more as a postural controller — maintaining body configuration — than as a true balance controller that responds to the direction of whole-body falling.

At the brainstem level, responses occur at approximately 100 ms. Brainstem circuits integrate a broader set of sensory signals and can compute the direction of whole-body motion, enabling coordinated muscle activity across multiple joints simultaneously. Rather than responding to stretch at one joint, brainstem-level control selects the muscle strategy appropriate for the actual falling direction. These circuits are shared between standing balance and walking — the center-of-mass control principles demonstrated in standing perturbation research generalize to corrective torques during perturbed gait, which is why standing balance is a tractable model for studying locomotor control.

At the cortical level, responses arrive later still and reflect higher-order assessment: Is the current strategy working? Should a different strategy be adopted — a step, a grab for support, a change of stance? Cortical involvement signals a shift from automatic to deliberate control and is associated with the kind of flexibility needed when environments change in unexpected ways.

A clarifying example illustrates why brainstem-level control matters. When a platform translates backward, the calf muscle is stretched and a stretch reflex activates it — a correct response for resisting forward sway. However, if the platform tilts forward (rotating the body backward), continuing to activate the calf would worsen the backward fall. Instead, the brainstem-level circuit reverses the command: the calf shuts off and the tibialis anterior activates. This switch requires information about whole-body falling direction, which is why it occurs at the brainstem time scale and cannot be accomplished by spinal proprioceptive reflexes alone.

Co-contraction — simultaneous activation of opposing muscles around a joint — is a postural strategy, not a balance strategy. It can stabilize joints against small perturbations but fails when a large tilt reverses the mechanical demand. Older adults show more co-contraction than younger adults; expert dancers show less. When expert ballet dancers are compared with novices on beam-walking tasks, dancers use the same muscle coordination patterns (synergies) for reactive balance, beam-walking, and overground walking — a pattern consistent with brainstem-level automatic organization even during a challenging task. Novices use more task-specific patterns, consistent with greater cortical engagement. People with better walking ability following stroke similarly show more synergy sharing across tasks, suggesting that automatic brainstem organization is a marker of more recovered motor function.

An important consideration for balance assessment is that faster return to baseline after a perturbation is not necessarily a sign of better balance. A stiffened, co-contracting body may return quickly from small perturbations but is poorly equipped to handle large or directionally varied ones — the perturbations that actually cause falls. A more meaningful measure is robustness: how large a perturbation can a person handle without stepping or falling? Rapid return driven by co-contraction does not translate to robust balance, and can be confounded with truly effective corrective control if speed of return is treated as the primary metric.

Brain Activity During Balance: Beta Oscillations and the N1 Potential

Direct measurement of cortical activity during balance experiments reveals two distinct signals that track individual differences in balance performance.

Pre-perturbation beta oscillations (approximately 13–30 Hz) predict individual differences in perceptual thresholds for whole-body motion direction. Participants with higher beta power in the moments before a perturbation tends to show worse perceptual thresholds. Beta oscillations are thought to reflect a state of reduced sensory receptivity — elevated beta may indicate that the brain is less open to incoming sensory information at that moment. This relationship holds on a trial-by-trial basis: when a burst of beta activity occurs just before a perturbation, discrimination accuracy is lower on that trial. Parkinson’s disease is associated with pathologically elevated beta oscillations in sensorimotor circuits — one of the most replicated findings in movement neuroscience — and this elevated baseline beta may contribute to the impaired whole-body motion perception and balance deficits seen in the disease. People with Parkinson’s also show elevated resting beta during ordinary standing (not just during perceptual tasks), and this elevated resting beta correlates with clinical balance scores.

The N1 potential is a negative voltage deflection of approximately 50 microvolts recorded from the scalp over the supplementary motor area — a region involved in sequential motor planning, located anterior to the primary motor cortex. This amplitude is notably large for a scalp EEG signal. Cortical evoked potentials to balance perturbations have been described in the research literature since the 1980s, and the N1 is interpreted as a balance-error assessment signal reflecting how challenging a perturbation is for that individual.

Several factors modulate N1 amplitude. It is larger when a perturbation is surprising, when a person has fear of falling, or when the perturbation is objectively more severe. It is smaller when the perturbation is anticipated or when attention is divided. In a paired-perturbation paradigm — where a second perturbation follows the first by exactly five seconds — younger adults show a reliably smaller N1 for the second, temporally predictable perturbation. Older adults show less modulation between the two responses. Preliminary data from ongoing research in people with Parkinson’s disease suggests the N1 may remain equally large for both perturbations regardless of predictability, as if each perturbation is equally surprising no matter how many times it has occurred. Over a series of 100 or more perturbations, young adults and older adults show gradual attenuation of the N1 as the stimulus becomes familiar; in some people with Parkinson’s disease or stroke, the response remains flat from the first to the last perturbation — no adaptation at all. This failure to modulate based on temporal context or repetition is another expression of the neural inflexibility that characterizes Parkinson’s disease more broadly.

In young adults, a larger N1 amplitude during a routine standing perturbation predicts worse performance on a beam-walking task — a task that requires the same balance system under greater mechanical challenge. In older adults, a larger N1 correlates with lower balance confidence by self-report and with slower cognitive set-shifting performance on the Trail Making Test. Because it quantifies the subjective challenge posed by the same objective perturbation to different individuals, the N1 may provide a principled way to identify the appropriate training difficulty for each person — calibrating challenge so it is neither too easy (no learning) nor too hard (failure and risk of injury).

The N1 shows good test-retest reliability across sessions separated by one week, and in some individuals by up to one year — a prerequisite for any useful clinical biomarker. This reliability opens the possibility of tracking whether balance health is changing over time, whether a rehabilitation intervention is shifting the neural challenge index in the intended direction, and whether early changes in balance-related brain activity precede detectable deficits on standard clinical tests.

Cognitive Set Shifting and Motor Inflexibility

Executive functions — higher cognitive processes governing attention, planning, and flexible responding — interact meaningfully with motor control for balance. Cognitive set shifting, specifically the ability to update behavioral strategy when the rules of a situation change, is one such function, and impairment in it is associated with fall risk in older adults and people with Parkinson’s disease.

Set shifting is measured clinically using the Trail Making Test. In Part A, participants connect numbered dots in ascending order, assessing basic attention and motor speed. In Part B, they alternate between numbers and letters (1–A–2–B–3–C…), requiring continuous switching between two response rules. Because both parts involve the same number of targets, the additional time needed for Part B beyond Part A is attributed specifically to the cognitive cost of rule-switching. The trail-making test is easily administered with pen and paper in a few minutes, making it practical for clinical and research settings. In older adult and clinical populations, it reliably captures meaningful variation in set-shifting ability. For healthy young adults, however, the task may not be sufficiently challenging to reveal performance differences, and computerized versions with adaptive difficulty or faster stimulus timing may be more sensitive in that population.

The relevance of cognitive set shifting to balance lies in its motor counterpart: motor set shifting is the ability to update muscle coordination patterns when the biomechanical context changes. Research by Fay Horak and colleagues demonstrated this directly. When people stand on a platform and are perturbed backward, a characteristic posterior muscle chain response is elicited. If they are immediately seated and perturbed again in the same direction, healthy individuals rapidly update their responses — lower-limb muscle activity is attenuated and trunk muscles appropriate for seated balance activate instead. People with Parkinson’s disease, however, maintain the lower-limb muscle timing appropriate for standing even in the seated condition, activating muscles that contribute nothing to seated balance correction. Their motor system fails to update its strategy for the changed postural context. This is the motor parallel of the Trail Making Test deficit: just as their cognition fails to switch rules, their motor system fails to switch coordination strategies.

The direction of this motor set-shifting failure also helps explain why it is observed experimentally: participants are moved from the more demanding context (standing) to the less demanding one (seated). In this direction, there is no immediate negative consequence of maintaining the standing muscle pattern — it does not actively impair seated balance, so no error feedback prompts correction. Going from seated to standing would likely produce faster adjustment because the motor system would receive clear negative feedback if it persisted in using a pattern inappropriate for the new demands.

In older adults with no overt clinical deficits, impaired cognitive set shifting (slower Trail Making Test B) correlates with greater muscle co-contraction during balance perturbations. This co-contraction results in smaller center-of-mass displacement after a perturbation — which may appear superficially like better balance — but is the hallmark of a postural stiffening strategy rather than directed corrective control. In these same individuals, the tibialis anterior muscle is inappropriately activated following backward perturbations at a cortical time window — delayed beyond when brainstem-level inhibition should have been sufficient. This suggests that these individuals are not simply relying on a different motor strategy by choice; they appear unable to distinguish forward from backward perturbation direction with enough confidence to appropriately gate the tibialis anterior response.

The overall picture is of a nervous system that cannot efficiently switch its strategy based on context — and this motor inflexibility appears to share underlying mechanisms with cognitive inflexibility as measured on standardized tests. Because balance control in aging increasingly relies on cortical resources shared with executive function, balance assessment should not be limited to purely motor measures. Cognitive assessments, particularly those probing set shifting, provide complementary information that may predict fall risk above and beyond what motor tests reveal.
