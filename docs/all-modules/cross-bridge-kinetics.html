Cross-bridge kinetics & experimental system analysis methods

Keywords

cross-bridge cycling, demembranated fibers, sinusoidal analysis, force-length relationship, length-dependent activation

Learning objectives

  1. Describe the major biochemical steps of the actomyosin cross-bridge cycle and explain how phosphate release, ADP release, and ATP binding govern force production and cross-bridge detachment.
  2. Explain how sinusoidal length perturbation analysis and step-stretch protocols are used to estimate cross-bridge attachment and detachment rates in demembranated fiber preparations, and interpret the elastic and viscous modulus outputs in terms of the ABC process model.
  3. Relate sarcomere length-dependent changes in cross-bridge kinetics to the cellular basis of the Frank-Starling mechanism and discuss how myosin isoform composition influences the magnitude of this length dependence.

Overview

Experimental preparations

Demembranated (chemically skinned) muscle fiber preparations are produced by removing the cell membrane, allowing the intracellular environment to be controlled entirely by the bathing solution—calcium concentration, ATP level, and ionic strength can be set independently. For cardiac studies, multicellular myocardial strips (roughly 100–500 µm wide and 400–1200 µm long) are dissected from ventricular tissue; for single skeletal fiber experiments, fibers are trimmed to approximately 2–3 mm in length with diameters in the range of 50–120 µm. Each preparation is mounted between a force transducer and a length-controlling motor, and sarcomere length is set directly by imaging the regular thick-and-thin-filament striations under light microscopy.

The cross-bridge cycle

Calcium binding to troponin C initiates thin filament activation by shifting tropomyosin from the blocked to the open state, exposing myosin-binding sites on actin (McKillop & Geeves, 1993). Myosin attaches to actin in a post-hydrolysis M·ADP·Pi state; Pi release is functionally coupled to the force-generating power stroke, though whether lever-arm rotation precedes or follows Pi exit remains under investigation (Lymn & Taylor, 1971; Dantzig et al., 1992; Woody et al., 2019). ADP release from the AM·ADP complex is the rate-limiting step for cross-bridge detachment and is strain-dependent—mechanical load slows ADP exit and prolongs the strongly-bound state, providing a mechanism by which force modulates cycling rate (Siemankowski et al., 1985; Nyitrai & Geeves, 2004). ATP binding to the rigor complex then causes rapid myosin detachment, followed by ATP hydrolysis to restore the high-actin-affinity pre-stroke state, completing the cycle.

Passive mechanics and the force-length relationship

Striated muscle generates both active (calcium-activated) force and passive (resting) force; the primary intracellular source of passive tension is titin, which dominates at moderate sarcomere lengths, while collagen in the perimysium and endomysium becomes the major contributor at longer lengths (Granzier & Irving, 1995; Wang et al., 1991). The active force-length relationship follows the classic Gordon-Huxley-Julian description: force rises along an ascending limb as thick-thin filament overlap increases, reaches a plateau at optimal overlap, then declines along a descending limb as overlap is lost at very long sarcomere lengths (Gordon et al., 1966). Total force at any sarcomere length is the sum of these active and passive contributions.

Length-dependent activation and the Frank-Starling mechanism

At the organ level, the Frank-Starling law describes how greater ventricular filling increases stroke volume; the cellular basis is length-dependent activation, in which increasing sarcomere length within the physiological range enhances both peak active force and calcium sensitivity—a leftward shift of the force-pCa curve—independent of changes in filament overlap (Kentish et al., 1986; Allen & Kentish, 1985). Cross-bridge kinetics contribute to this length dependence: at longer sarcomere lengths and higher internal forces, ADP release from the AM·ADP complex slows, prolonging cross-bridge attachment and increasing force per cross-bridge, likely because mechanical strain on the cross-bridge alters the geometry of the catalytic pocket. Conversely, shorter sarcomere lengths are associated with faster cross-bridge detachment, which may facilitate relaxation. Together, these sarcomere length-dependent changes in ADP release rate represent a kinetic contribution to the Frank-Starling mechanism that operates alongside calcium sensitivity and filament overlap effects.

Myosin isoforms and cross-bridge kinetic sensitivity

The predominant cardiac myosin isoform varies with body size: alpha-myosin heavy chain (alpha-MHC, MYH6), with its high ATPase rate and fast shortening velocity, dominates in small animals such as mice and rats, while beta-myosin heavy chain (beta-MHC, MYH7)—a slower, higher-economy isoform—predominates in larger animals including humans (Lompre et al., 1979). The magnitude of length-dependent kinetic sensitivity differs markedly with isoform: in skeletal muscle, slow-twitch (type I / beta-MHC) fibers show greater length-dependent calcium sensitivity than fast-twitch fibers, while fast-twitch myosin kinetics are comparatively insensitive to changes in sarcomere length. This isoform-dependent variation in length sensitivity may partly explain why the cellular expression of the Frank-Starling mechanism differs across species.

System analysis: sinusoidal perturbation, step-stretch, and the ABC model

In sinusoidal length perturbation analysis, the muscle is oscillated through a range of frequencies while force is recorded; the in-phase and out-of-phase components of the force response yield the elastic and viscous moduli, respectively, mapping kinetic processes that operate at different time scales (Kawai & Brandt, 1980). Step-stretch protocols apply a rapid length step and record the resulting multi-phase force transient—comprising a rapid elastic force rise (Phase 1), fast tension decay as stretched cross-bridges detach (Phase 2), and slower tension recovery as new cross-bridges recruit (Phase 3)—providing estimates of cross-bridge attachment and detachment kinetics from a shorter activation period (Huxley & Simmons, 1971; Ford, Huxley, & Simmons, 1977). Frequency-domain sinusoidal data are typically fit to the three-component ABC model of Kawai and Brandt (1980), in which Process A reflects slow regulatory dynamics, Process B reflects cross-bridge recruitment, and Process C reflects the force-generating kinetic step, providing estimates of Pi-release rates and cross-bridge attachment time. MATLAB scripts developed in the Tanner lab implement fitting routines for both sinusoidal and step-stretch data, extracting ABC process parameters and estimating ADP release and ATP binding rates across fiber types and species.
