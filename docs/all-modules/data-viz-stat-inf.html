Data visualization and statistical inference for biomechanics

Keywords

Anscombe’s quartet, data visualization, p-values, statistical power, effect size

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, learners will be able to:

  1. Explain why descriptive statistics alone can be misleading and why visualizing data at every stage of analysis is essential for drawing valid conclusions.
  2. Describe what a p-value represents, how the false positive rate relates to the conventional significance threshold of p < 0.05, and why statistical significance does not imply practical importance.
  3. Identify how sample size, statistical power, and effect size are related, and recognize that small studies are best positioned to detect large effects.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. James Finley

Overview

Research goals and sources of variability

Dr. Finley opened by framing the workshop around a central question posed to participants: what is the primary goal of scientists? Responses converged on themes of answering questions, generating knowledge, and understanding the world. He then outlined four broad categories of research: descriptive studies that characterize populations; methods-development studies focused on the validity and reliability of measurement tools; studies of relationships between variables using tools such as correlation and regression; and experimental studies aimed at establishing cause-and-effect relationships.

A recurring theme was variability. Finley identified three types relevant to research design. Concomitant variation — the change in a dependent variable when the independent variable is manipulated — is the variability that enables causal inference. Extraneous variability, introduced by inconsistent measurement across sessions, imprecise or inaccurate instruments, or uncontrolled participant characteristics such as fluctuating alertness, reduces the ability to detect true effects. Confounding variables constitute a third source that can prevent researchers from establishing causality altogether. He described statistics as tools for both quantifying and explaining these sources of variability.

Anscombe’s Quartet: the limits of summary statistics

To illustrate the dangers of relying on summary statistics alone, Finley walked participants through Anscombe’s Quartet — four data sets that are visually very different but share nearly identical statistical properties. Using a MATLAB script provided in a shared Google Drive folder, participants computed the mean and standard deviation of each data set’s X and Y values, then fit a linear regression to each, obtaining slope, intercept, and R-squared values. In every case, the values were essentially identical across all four data sets. Yet when the data are plotted, it is immediately clear that a linear model is only appropriate for the first data set; the others show curvature, outliers, or degenerate structure that makes the fitted line meaningless.

The key take-home from this exercise was stated directly: looking at summary statistics alone — whether descriptive statistics such as mean and standard deviation, or inferential statistics such as R-squared — does not reveal how the underlying data actually look. This problem is especially pronounced with small samples, which are common in biomechanics, human, and animal model research. Finley’s recommendation was to visualize data early and often throughout the entire analysis pipeline, from the moment of data collection through each subsequent processing step.

Making publication-ready figures in MATLAB

Finley presented a workflow for creating figures he described as publication-ready, illustrated using a second MATLAB script applied to the same Anscombe’s Quartet data. A central comparison in this section was between a bar plot with error bars and a dot plot showing individual data points and medians. When displayed as a bar chart, all four Anscombe data sets appeared similar in spread. When displayed as a dot plot, differences in the distribution and the presence of outliers became immediately apparent. This reinforced the earlier point that the choice of visualization method itself can either reveal or conceal the structure of data.

Specific recommendations for figure formatting included: always labeling axes with full words and units; using MATLAB’s set function to control properties such as face color, edge color, line width, and font size in code rather than by post-processing in a separate illustration program; and specifying figure dimensions in centimeters so that the exported file matches the intended print size (for example, one and a half columns in a standard two-column journal format). He recommended saving figures in multiple formats: a MATLAB .fig file for later in-program editing, a PDF for annotation or presentation, and a TIFF for journal submission, noting that TIFF files preserve more image information than JPEG, which uses compression. He also noted that sub-panel labels (A, B, etc.) can be added directly in MATLAB code using the text command, specifying X and Y coordinates for label placement. His general principle was to encode all figure formatting choices in code so that figures can be regenerated automatically when analyses are revised.

Understanding p-values through simulation

The third major section addressed inferential statistics and the meaning of p-values. Finley introduced a simulated experiment designed to make the concept concrete: 1,000 independent studies each tested whether drinking one ounce of water improved long-jump performance in Olympic male athletes. Because the intervention has no plausible effect on jump distance, the null hypothesis was known to be true by construction. In the simulation, each study drew 100 participants from a control population with a mean long-jump distance of 8 meters and a standard deviation of 20 cm, and 100 participants from an identically specified test population, then compared the two groups using a t-test.

Across 1,000 simulations, the resulting p-values followed a roughly uniform distribution between 0 and 1. The false positive rate — the proportion of experiments that returned p < 0.05 despite the null hypothesis being true — was approximately 0.048, close to 0.05. Finley used this demonstration to give concrete meaning to the standard definition: the p-value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the observed one if the null hypothesis were true. A p-value does not indicate how important or scientifically meaningful a finding is.

Finley also referenced a 2015 paper titled “Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science,” in which a large group of researchers attempted to replicate findings from prominent psychology journals. In the original published studies, nearly all had p-values below 0.05; in the replications, the median p-value was above 0.2, and only a small percentage of studies replicated at the conventional threshold. He placed this paper in the shared folder for participants to read. During the Q&A portion of the session, Finley noted that p-values are also influenced by sample size: with very large data sets, p < 0.05 is almost always obtained, but this only means that very small effects can be detected — not that those effects are meaningful.

Sample size, statistical power, and effect size

In the final section, Finley introduced the concepts of sample size and statistical power. He explained that MATLAB includes a built-in function for computing required sample size given a specified statistical test, the expected distribution under the null hypothesis, the expected difference between groups (effect size), and the desired power level. As an illustrative example, he described a study comparing vertical jump height between two groups of students, where pilot data showed a mean jump of 50 cm with a standard deviation of 4 cm. To detect a group difference of 2 cm with adequate power, the function returned a required sample size of 34 individuals per group.

Finley described the general relationships between these quantities: higher desired power requires a larger sample; smaller effect sizes also require larger samples to detect. Conversely, when researchers are limited in the participants or observations they can collect, they are best positioned to study effects that are likely to be large. He also drew a distinction between statistical significance and practical importance, noting that “significant” in everyday language implies importance, which is not guaranteed by p < 0.05. Effect sizes and their uncertainty — rather than p-values alone — were described as the more meaningful quantities to report and interpret.

During the Q&A portion, a participant asked how researchers determine what effect size is interesting. Finley noted that in clinical research, practitioners sometimes define a minimally clinically important difference — for example, a 0.4 meters per second change in walking speed, which might allow a person to cross a street before a traffic light changes. He acknowledged that equivalent benchmarks are rarely available in basic science research, and that researchers must make and defend their own judgments about what effect magnitudes matter, considering what a difference would enable functionally. He also mentioned confidence intervals and Bayesian methods as additional tools for characterizing uncertainty in effect estimates beyond the p-value.
