EMG filtering

Keywords

electromyography, EMG filtering, Butterworth filter, frequency analysis, linear envelope

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish between high pass, low pass, and band pass filters, and identify when each type is appropriate for EMG data.
  • Describe the two key filter parameters — cutoff frequency and filter order — and explain the trade-off between filter sharpness and artifact risk.
  • Outline the standard EMG processing pipeline: apply a high pass or band pass filter to raw data, rectify, and optionally create a smooth linear envelope.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Monica Daley

Overview

Introduction to Filter Theory

Digital filters exploit the fact that any signal can be represented as a sum of sine waves at different frequencies and amplitudes, selectively attenuating components in certain frequency bands while preserving others. The preserved frequency range is the pass band; the attenuated range is the stop band. Before selecting filter settings, examining the raw signal’s frequency composition via a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) — comparing a baseline segment to an active segment — reveals where real signal energy lies and where noise peaks occur, and should inform filter design rather than simply copying settings from the literature.

Filter Types: High Pass, Low Pass, and Band Pass

A high-pass filter attenuates low-frequency components while preserving high-frequency ones, making it appropriate for removing DC offset and motion artifact from raw EMG. A low-pass filter does the reverse and is used to smooth a rectified EMG signal into an activation envelope. A band-pass filter combines both cutoffs — preserving a defined frequency window and discarding energy above and below it — and is appropriate when noise exists at both the low and high ends of the spectrum.

Common Sources of EMG Noise and the Physiological Signal Range

Motor unit action potentials span roughly 20–450 Hz, and this range guides EMG filter design. The principal noise sources are motion artifact (typically below 20–25 Hz; De Luca, Gilmore & Kuznetsov, 2010), AC electrical line noise (60 Hz in North America, 50 Hz elsewhere), cardiac artifact in recordings from muscles near the chest, and — in combined recordings — interference from adjacent instruments such as sonomicrometry systems at frequencies that depend on the system’s pulse repetition rate. Because the lower boundary of the physiological signal range overlaps with some noise sources, filter settings should be chosen from inspection of the data rather than copied from the literature without verification.

Filter Parameters: Cutoff Frequency and Filter Order

The cutoff frequency marks the transition between pass band and stop band; in MATLAB, it is specified normalized to the Nyquist frequency (sampling frequency ÷ 2). Filter order controls roll-off sharpness: higher orders produce steeper transitions but risk introducing ringing artifacts — transient oscillations that can push a filtered signal below zero in regions that are clearly positive in the raw data. MATLAB’s filtfilt function applies the filter forward and then backward for zero-phase filtering, which prevents time shifts in the filtered signal but doubles the effective filter order relative to the design specification.

Practical EMG Filtering in MATLAB

MATLAB’s Butterworth filter workflow uses buttord to determine the minimum order and cutoff frequency meeting specified performance criteria, butter to compute filter coefficients, and filtfilt for zero-phase application. For a high-pass filter, single stop-band and pass-band frequencies are provided; for a band-pass filter, vectors of two frequencies are provided for each parameter, and providing two values automatically configures MATLAB’s Butterworth functions for bandpass mode. After applying any filter, the raw and filtered signals should always be plotted together to verify that peak shape is preserved and that no ringing artifacts have been introduced; hardware filtering already applied by the data acquisition system can reduce the need for aggressive software filter settings.

Creating a Smooth Linear Envelope

After high-pass or band-pass filtering, the EMG signal is rectified (absolute value of each sample) to convert it to a fully positive representation. A second, low-pass filter applied to the rectified signal produces the smooth linear envelope most commonly shown in published figures; a widely used implementation is a 4th-order Butterworth at 10 Hz (Winter, 2009), achieved with a 2nd-order specification when using filtfilt. The high-pass or band-pass filter must always be applied to the raw, un-rectified signal first — applying it after rectification can introduce spurious negative deflections not present in the original data.

Surface EMG Considerations and Electrode Configuration

Surface EMG is typically sampled at approximately 1,000 Hz in line with SENIAM guidelines (Hermens et al., 2000), whereas indwelling electrodes often use higher sampling rates to capture a broader frequency range. Surface recordings are susceptible to crosstalk from adjacent muscles, and signal characteristics vary with electrode placement relative to the motor end plate; for precise frequency characterization at the individual muscle level, indwelling electrodes provide cleaner isolation. High-density electrode arrays — multiple electrodes in a grid configuration — offer a middle ground by enabling spatial decomposition of EMG components, analogous to spatial filtering in EEG.
