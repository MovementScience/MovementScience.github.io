Intro to the mechanics of the musculoskeletal system

Keywords

stress-strain, viscoelasticity, connective tissue, joint mechanics, bone

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, students should be able to:

  1. Define stress, strain, and Young’s modulus, and explain how these quantities characterize the mechanical behavior of musculoskeletal tissues.
  2. Identify the main connective tissue types in the musculoskeletal system — bone, cartilage, tendon, and ligament — and describe how their microstructure determines their mechanical properties.
  3. Classify joint degrees of freedom and describe how internal loads (tension, compression, bending, torsion) are distributed within biological structures.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Katie Knaus

Overview

Mechanics and its divisions

Mechanics is the field of studying how bodies react to forces acting on them. Within mechanics, two major divisions are relevant to the musculoskeletal system.

In statics, bodies are in equilibrium: the forces acting on them are balanced, and there is no movement — velocity and acceleration are both zero. A person holding a fixed posture represents a static problem in which each body segment is in equilibrium.

In dynamics, the body or its segments are moving. Dynamics is further divided into kinematics, which describes the geometric aspects of motion — position, velocity, and acceleration — without regard to the forces causing that motion, and kinetics, which incorporates the forces responsible for motion.

Loads acting on the body include body weight (mass reacting to gravity), ground reaction forces and contact forces from the environment, and forces from interacting objects such as a handheld weight. When these loads balance, equilibrium is achieved and there is no movement. When they become unbalanced, the body accelerates.

Body segments, joints, and degrees of freedom

The human body is usefully modeled as a system of rigid segments connected at joints. Each segment has its own center of mass and associated inertia; the loads acting on individual segments — and the internal loads at joint interfaces — determine both posture and movement.

Joints define how adjacent segments can move relative to each other. Two key descriptors are degrees of freedom (DOF) — the number of independent ways two segments can move relative to one another — and range of motion, the extent of movement permitted by the joint structures. DOF can be rotational (movement about an axis) or translational (displacement along an axis).

The hip is a ball-and-socket joint providing 3 rotational DOF: flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and internal/external rotation. The elbow is often simplified as a hinge joint: the humeroulnar articulation behaves approximately as a hinge with 1 DOF (flexion/extension). This is a useful model simplification, but the full elbow complex has 2 functional DOF when forearm rotation is included, because the proximal radioulnar joint — anatomically part of the elbow — adds pronation/supination as a second independent motion. The wrist (radiocarpal joint) has 2 DOF — flexion/extension and radial/ulnar deviation. Forearm pronation/supination occurs at the proximal and distal radioulnar joints and is not a DOF of the wrist joint itself.

By constraining certain DOF, joints generate reaction forces and moments in the directions that motion is not permitted. This is why free body diagrams of individual segments include joint reaction forces and moments as unknowns to be solved from equilibrium.

Internal loads and stress states

When external loads act on a body segment, internal loads develop within the structure. Cutting a cross-section through the structure and applying equilibrium reveals three types of internal load: a normal force perpendicular to the cut surface, shear forces parallel to the cut surface, and moments (bending and torsional) about the cross-sectional axes.

These internal loads produce characteristic stress distributions:

  • Tension and compression (axial normal force): stress is distributed uniformly across the cross-section, directed perpendicular to the cut surface.
  • Bending (from a bending moment): the greatest tensile stress develops at the outer fiber on the tension side, the greatest compressive stress at the outer fiber on the compression side, and stress is zero at the neutral axis in the middle. A bone bending under load is longer and in tension on one side, shorter and in compression on the other — the bone cross-section illustrates this distribution directly.
  • Torsion (from a torsional moment about the longitudinal axis): shear stress is greatest at the outer surface of the structure and approaches zero at the center, because the outermost material must displace the most during twisting.

Stress (σ) is force distributed over an area — in its simplest uniaxial form, σ = F/A. Strain (ε) is the change in length normalized by the original length (ε = ΔL/L₀), a dimensionless quantity often expressed as a percentage. These normalizations allow material behavior to be described independently of specimen geometry.

Material properties of musculoskeletal tissues

In the linear elastic (Hookean) region, stress is proportional to strain: σ = Eε, where Young’s modulus (E) is the proportionality constant and equals the slope of the linear portion of the stress-strain curve. A higher Young’s modulus indicates a stiffer material that requires greater stress to achieve the same deformation.

Musculoskeletal connective tissues display a characteristic nonlinear stress-strain curve with four regions: a low-stiffness toe region, a stiffer linear elastic region, a yield and micro-failure region where progressive structural damage occurs, and ultimate failure. The yield strength and ultimate strength mark the stress levels at which these transitions occur.

Two additional features distinguish biological tissues from idealized isotropic elastic materials:

  • Anisotropy: mechanical properties differ depending on the direction of loading, owing to the preferential alignment of collagen fibers and mineral crystallites within the tissue. Loading a tendon along its fiber axis versus across it yields dramatically different stress-strain curves.
  • Viscoelasticity: the stress-strain response depends on the rate of loading (strain rate). Under rapid loading, tissues appear stiffer than under slow loading. Viscoelasticity also manifests as creep, stress relaxation, and hysteresis — behaviors arising from fluid-solid interactions and intrinsic polymer chain dynamics within the tissue.

The structural mechanical properties of a specific anatomical structure depend on both material properties and morphology. A longer ligament with the same material properties and cross-sectional area is less stiff than a shorter one (stiffness k = EA/L); a ligament with a larger cross-sectional area requires greater force to produce the same elongation and is therefore stiffer. Stress and strain normalizations account for these morphological differences when characterizing material properties independent of specimen size.

Bone and cartilage

Bone exists in two tissue forms. Compact (cortical) bone forms the dense outer shell of bones. Trabecular (cancellous) bone forms the inner, porous structure — particularly at the ends of long bones — with an open lattice of bony struts (trabeculae) that distributes load while minimizing mass. At the microscale, cortical bone is organized into osteons (Haversian systems): concentric rings (lamellae) of collagen fibers with mineral content surrounding central canals that house blood vessels and nerve fibers. This oriented microstructure gives bone its anisotropic mechanical properties.

There are three types of cartilage. Articular (hyaline) cartilage lines joint surfaces and reduces friction at articulations; its friction coefficient is among the lowest of any biological surface, achieved through fluid pressurization and glycoprotein boundary lubricants. Elastic cartilage, distinguished by its high elastin content, is found in structures such as the external ear. Fibrocartilage is a dense, stiff tissue found in the menisci and the labra (acetabular and glenoid). Intervertebral discs are composite structures: the annulus fibrosus is fibrocartilaginous, while the nucleus pulposus is a hydrated proteoglycan-rich gel — not fibrocartilage — with a distinct mechanical function of distributing compressive loads through hydrostatic pressure.

The mechanical behavior of cartilage arises largely from its extracellular matrix. Proteoglycans (primarily aggrecan) are hydrophilic macromolecules bearing dense negative fixed charges that attract and retain interstitial fluid through Donnan osmotic pressure. Fluid pressurization under load, together with the tensile load-bearing capacity of the collagen fiber network, is the basis of the viscoelastic behavior characteristic of cartilage. The cell density of connective tissue also governs remodeling capacity: fibrocartilage has low cell density and limited vascularity, which is why injuries to structures such as the meniscus heal slowly.

Tendon, ligament, and muscle mechanics

Tendons connect muscle to bone; ligaments connect bone to bone. Both are dense fibrous connective tissues composed primarily of type I collagen. In tendons, collagen fibers are arranged nearly parallel to one another, optimizing uniaxial tensile load transmission along the tendon axis. In ligaments, fibers are also primarily longitudinally aligned but show slightly greater inter-bundle angular variation, reflecting the multidirectional loading demands of structures that stabilize joints.

The low-stiffness toe region of the tendon and ligament stress-strain curve arises from the progressive recruitment and straightening of initially crimped (wavy) collagen fibers under load. As strain increases, more fibers straighten and begin to bear load, progressively increasing tissue stiffness until all fibers are engaged and the tissue enters the linear elastic region. Beyond the linear region, fibers begin to fail sequentially, reducing stiffness until ultimate failure.

Muscle has passive and active mechanical properties, both characterized empirically. Passive behavior — force generated by a lengthened but unactivated muscle — arises from the extracellular matrix (ECM) and intracellular proteins. Titin, the giant elastic protein spanning the sarcomere’s I-band, is the dominant intracellular contributor to passive stiffness; titin also contributes to active force through calcium-dependent interactions with actin. Active force is generated by the sarcomeric contractile proteins (actin and myosin). The active force-length relationship, force-velocity relationship, and passive force-length curve are the core empirical descriptors of muscle mechanics; they cannot be derived analytically from first principles and are parameterized from experimental data.

With aging, increased ECM content (collagen crosslinking and accumulation) and intramuscular fat infiltration alter passive mechanical properties and reduce contractile tissue fraction. Muscle hypertrophy occurs through an increase in muscle fiber cross-sectional area — expansion of existing fibers through accretion of myofibrillar proteins — rather than through a meaningful increase in fiber number.

Experimental characterization and computational modeling

Because the material properties of musculoskeletal tissues arise from their microstructure, they cannot generally be derived analytically; they are characterized empirically through mechanical testing. A typical approach applies controlled deformation to a tissue specimen using a uniaxial testing machine while measuring force, then converts force to stress and elongation to strain to construct stress-strain curves. Tissue samples can be loaded along or across the primary fiber direction to capture anisotropic behavior, or tested at different loading rates to characterize viscoelastic rate-dependence.

Testing configurations range from ex vivo specimens (cadaveric or animal tissue removed from its native context) to in situ setups that preserve more physiologically relevant boundary conditions and combined load states. Converting measured force and elongation to stress and strain requires accurate measurement of cross-sectional area and original length — a nontrivial requirement for irregularly shaped biological structures.

Where direct mechanical measurement is impractical, computational modeling allows forces and stresses to be estimated under complex loading conditions. FEBio (Finite Elements for Biomechanics) is an open-source finite element solver designed specifically for biological materials, supporting hyperelastic, viscoelastic, biphasic, and multiphasic constitutive formulations suited to musculoskeletal tissues (Maas et al., 2012, PMID 22482660). Constitutive models — the mathematical definitions of nonlinear, anisotropic, viscoelastic tissue behavior — require high-fidelity experimental measurements as inputs, linking the empirical characterization of tissue properties directly to computational prediction of in vivo mechanics.
