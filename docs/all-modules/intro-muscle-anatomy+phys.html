Introduction to muscle anatomy and physiology

Keywords

sarcomere, titin, excitation-contraction coupling, force-velocity relationship, muscle architecture

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, participants should be able to:

  1. Describe the hierarchical organization of skeletal muscle from the whole organ to the sarcomere level, including the roles of key structural proteins—actin, myosin, and titin—in generating and regulating force.
  2. Explain the fundamental muscle properties (excitation-contraction coupling, force-length, force-velocity, and force-frequency relationships), identify how each is measured, and articulate the limitations of applying static or quasi-static measurements to predict dynamic, natural movements.
  3. Evaluate how variation in muscle architecture, fiber type, sarcomere structure, and titin isoform expression tunes muscle function across muscles within an organism and across species.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Jenna Monroy

Overview

The Conserved and Versatile Muscular System

The muscular system is one of the most highly conserved across the animal kingdom, with the fundamental organization of muscle, mechanisms of force generation, and responses to activation shared from insects to large mammals (Burkholder & Lieber, 2001). Despite this conservation, muscles are functionally versatile: a single muscle can act as a motor generating mechanical work, a spring storing and returning elastic energy, a brake absorbing energy during active lengthening, or a strut transmitting force with minimal length change. Subtle differences in structure, protein isoform expression, architecture, or neural control can substantially alter whole-animal performance.

Sarcomere Structure and Hierarchical Organization

Skeletal muscle is organized hierarchically: the whole muscle comprises fascicles containing individual fibers, each fiber contains myofibrils, and each myofibril consists of repeating contractile units called sarcomeres. The sarcomere—bounded by Z-discs, with thin actin filaments extending inward and thick myosin filaments anchored at the M-line—is the fundamental unit of force generation; resting sarcomere length in vertebrates is typically 2.0–2.5 µm (Gordon et al., 1966; Burkholder & Lieber, 2001). A third filament, titin, spans from the Z-disc to the M-line and provides passive elastic resistance when the sarcomere is stretched (Labeit & Kolmerer, 1995). Invertebrate sarcomeres can differ substantially: obliquely striated muscle found in some soft-bodied animals can elongate far beyond the range of vertebrate sarcomeres while still generating force, a property called superelongation (Rosenbluth, 1967).

Excitation-Contraction Coupling

Excitation-contraction coupling (ECC) links an action potential at the muscle membrane to mechanical contraction: the action potential propagates into the T-tubule network, triggering calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptor channels, and the resulting rise in intracellular calcium causes troponin to expose myosin-binding sites on actin (Bers, 2002). Relaxation requires calcium to be pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA, an energy-requiring process that is slower than calcium release; this kinetic asymmetry allows successive stimuli to sum when they arrive before full relaxation—a process called temporal summation. ECC kinetics vary across fiber types: muscles that operate at high contraction frequencies have higher sarcoplasmic reticulum density and faster calcium cycling machinery than slow-twitch fibers (Berchtold et al., 2000).

Force-Frequency Relationship

Force output increases with stimulation frequency as successive twitches overlap before the muscle fully relaxes—a process called summation—culminating in fused tetanus, where force is smooth and maximal, when twitches overlap completely (Lieber, 2002). This relationship arises from calcium dynamics: higher frequencies allow calcium to accumulate in the cytoplasm, activating more cross-bridges and increasing force until a ceiling set by cross-bridge number and sarcomere geometry is reached. In intact animals, the nervous system modulates both stimulation frequency (rate coding) and motor unit recruitment to produce graded forces, and brief high-frequency bursts (doublet potentiation) can transiently enhance force beyond what steady-state measurements predict.

Force-Length Relationship

Active muscle force peaks at the optimal length (L₀), where cross-bridge overlap between thick and thin filaments is greatest, and declines at both shorter lengths (steric interference between filaments) and longer lengths (reduced overlap), following the classic Gordon-Huxley-Julian force-length curve (Gordon et al., 1966). Passive force, contributed primarily by titin and the extracellular matrix, increases progressively with length, so total muscle force at any length is the sum of active and passive contributions. Operating range varies across species: most vertebrate muscles function near the descending limb during natural movements, while some invertebrate muscles—including proposed super-contracting chameleon tongue retractor muscles—maintain force at sarcomere lengths far outside the vertebrate range (Herrel et al., 2001). The force-length relationship is a quasi-static, isometric measure; active stretching and shortening during natural movements can cause force to deviate substantially from this curve.

Force-Velocity Relationship and Muscle Architecture

Active force during shortening decreases with increasing velocity—the force-velocity relationship—reaching maximum at isometric conditions and falling to zero at maximum shortening velocity (V_max), a relationship reflecting cross-bridge cycling kinetics first characterized by Hill (1938). Power output—the product of force and velocity—peaks at approximately one-third of V_max in many vertebrate muscles and declines toward both extremes (Josephson, 1993); fast-twitch fibers achieve higher peak power than slow-twitch fibers because of their faster myosin ATPase activity and cross-bridge cycling rate. Muscle architecture—the arrangement of fibers relative to the line of action—determines the force and velocity characteristics of the whole muscle: parallel-fibered muscles maximize shortening speed and excursion by placing more sarcomeres in series, while pennate muscles pack more fibers into a given volume, increasing physiological cross-sectional area and maximum force capacity at some cost to shortening velocity (Lieber & Fridén, 2000). The force-velocity relationship is measured under isotonic, maximal-activation conditions and does not capture history-dependent effects, submaximal activation, or contributions from elastic structures such as titin.

Titin as a Tunable Viscoelastic Spring

Titin is the largest known protein, spanning continuously from the Z-disc to the M-line, and functions as the primary passive elastic element of the sarcomere (Labeit & Kolmerer, 1995). The extensible I-band region contains two mechanically distinct spring segments: a tandem immunoglobulin (Ig) domain region that is compliant at low forces and a PEVK domain that contributes the steeper, exponential rise in passive force at longer sarcomere lengths (Granzier & Labeit, 2004); both regions are sites of alternative splicing that generate isoforms of different lengths and stiffnesses, with longer isoforms (e.g., soleus) conferring lower passive stiffness and shorter isoforms (e.g., psoas) conferring higher stiffness. Evidence from in vitro experiments supports the hypothesis that the N2A element of titin can interact with actin in a calcium-dependent manner, potentially stiffening the sarcomere during active contraction and contributing to force during active stretch—a mechanism under active investigation that is not yet universally accepted (Dutta et al., 2018; Nishikawa, 2020). In invertebrates such as Drosophila, mechanical diversity is generated not through alternative splicing of a single titin gene but through expression of multiple distinct titin-like proteins, including projectin and sallimus (Burkart et al., 2007; Bullard et al., 2005).
