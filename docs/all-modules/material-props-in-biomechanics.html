Material properties in biomechanics

Keywords

viscoelasticity, titin, muscle mechanics, power amplification, sarcomere

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, you will be able to:

  1. Describe the mechanical properties of springs and dampers and explain how they are combined in basic viscoelastic models (Maxwell, Kelvin-Voigt, and Hill), including how each model distributes stress and strain between its elements.
  2. Explain how the protein titin contributes to the passive and active viscoelastic properties of skeletal muscle, and how two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series can represent those properties across a range of muscle lengths and activation levels.
  3. Describe how active skeletal muscle stores and releases elastic potential energy during stretch, and explain why activation dramatically increases both the amount of energy stored and the time over which it is retained.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Kiisa Nishikawa

Overview

Introduction: From Frog Tongues to Muscle Mechanics

Standard treatments of biomechanics material properties emphasize bone, cartilage, and tendon, but skeletal muscle is itself a material with distinctive and tunable mechanical properties. Projectile-tongue feeding in frogs and toads provides a vivid example of demanding energy requirements: the jaw-opening muscles reach maximum length when the mouth is closed, precluding active pre-stretch, yet must store and release energy at very high rates to capture fast-moving prey. Understanding where and how muscles store elastic energy — and how activation modifies that storage — is the central question addressed in this module.

Springs, Dampers, and the Foundations of Viscoelasticity

A linear spring obeys Hooke’s Law: force is proportional to displacement (F = kx), or in normalized form, stress equals elastic modulus times strain (σ = Eε). A damper (dashpot) generates force proportional to the rate of displacement rather than displacement itself (F = bẋ, where b is the damping constant). Both spring and damper properties scale consistently from the whole-muscle level down to individual filaments within the sarcomere, because they depend on material composition rather than specimen size.

Viscoelastic Models: From Two to Four Elements

Combining springs and dampers in different arrangements produces viscoelastic models with distinct force-time behaviors. The Maxwell model (spring and damper in series) produces an initial large force that decays to zero under a fixed strain; the Kelvin-Voigt model (spring and damper in parallel) produces a force that decays only to the residual elastic force in the spring. The Hill model places a spring in series with a Kelvin-Voigt element; the time constant τ = b/E of a Kelvin-Voigt element governs how quickly force decays after a perturbation, and more complex multi-element arrangements produce correspondingly richer behaviors.

Measuring Viscoelastic Properties: Three Experimental Approaches

Three experimental protocols are commonly used to characterize viscoelastic properties in muscle: ramp stretch-and-hold, sinusoidal perturbations, and white-noise perturbations. Sinusoidal protocols allow elastic and viscous contributions to be decomposed because elastic forces are in phase with the oscillation while viscous forces are 90 degrees out of phase; white-noise protocols probe multiple frequencies simultaneously and can reduce total experiment time. Kirsch, Boskov, and Rymer (1994, IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering) compared all three protocols in cat soleus and found that no single standard model fit both ramp-stretch and white-noise data simultaneously, with best-fitting models explaining only 80–85% of force variance.

System Identification in Mouse and Frog Muscles

Ramp-stretch experiments performed across the full physiological range of muscle lengths — both passively and at the plateau of isometric force — combined with system identification reveal the structure of the best-fitting viscoelastic model of muscle. A model consisting of two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series fits data from mouse soleus and three bullfrog muscles (sartorius, iliofibularis, semitendinosus) with high R-squared values and residuals on the order of 10⁻⁴. Comparison across three titin genotypes of mouse soleus — wild type, the Mdm spontaneous mutant (deletion of ~50 amino acids at the N2A/PEVK boundary), and a transgenic with exons 112–158 of PEVK titin deleted — provides independent evidence that titin structure drives differences between the two model elements.

Titin Subdomain Assignment and Activation-Dependent Tuning

The two Kelvin-Voigt elements identified by system identification (k1/b1 and k2/b2) differ in passive behavior and response to activation, pointing to distinct molecular origins within titin. The k1/b1 element, assigned to the PEVK1 and PEVK2 sub-domains near the Z-disc, behaves as an exponential spring whose stiffness varies over approximately three orders of magnitude across physiological length; upon activation, its time constant increases from ~100 ms to ~0.5–1 s, enabling energy retention across a full locomotor cycle. The k2/b2 element, assigned to the PEVK3 sub-domain near the M-line, stiffens and transitions from exponential to linear spring behavior upon activation while its time constant shortens to ~5 ms. This contrasting behavior means activation shifts energy storage predominantly into the slower k1/b1 element, dramatically extending the timescale over which elastic energy is retained relative to passive muscle.

Power, Energy Storage, and Comparison to Prior Literature

Elastic energy stored in each Kelvin-Voigt element can be estimated from system-identified stiffness values as ½kx² (equivalently ½F²/k), with approximately two-thirds of stored energy dissipating within one time constant. For frog sartorius, total power in the viscoelastic transmission rises from ~1 W/kg passively to ~100 W/kg upon activation; for semitendinosus, from ~8 W/kg to ~200 W/kg. Energy stored in active frog sartorius reaches approximately 0.063 P₀L₀, close to the 0.092 P₀L₀ total storage reported by Linari, Woledge, and Curtin (2003, Journal of Physiology) from single frog anterior tibialis fibers — work that found only approximately one-third of stored energy explicable by cross-bridges, thick filaments, thin filaments, and tendons combined.

Broader Implications and Extensions

Skeletal muscle functions as a tunable viscoelastic actuator that can harvest energy from environmental loads during stretch and release stored energy to amplify or redirect cross-bridge power output, with individual titin domains specialized for mechanical roles spanning orders of magnitude in frequency and force. The two-Kelvin-Voigt model is planned for extension to muscle-tendon units by adding a series spring representing the external tendon, and differences between fast and slow muscles in titin N2A actin-binding site location (closer to the Z-disc in EDL than in soleus) have been characterized as a fast-muscle high-gear configuration. Open-source Lagrangian dynamics models incorporating cross-bridge activation, N2A actin-binding dynamics, thin-filament titin winding, and length- and activation-dependent viscoelasticity are under development as replacements for Hill models in musculoskeletal simulations.
