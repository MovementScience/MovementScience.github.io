Material properties in biomechanics

Keywords

viscoelasticity, titin, muscle mechanics, power amplification, sarcomere

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, you will be able to:

  1. Describe the mechanical properties of springs and dampers and explain how they are combined in basic viscoelastic models (Maxwell, Kelvin-Voigt, and Hill), including how each model distributes stress and strain between its elements.
  2. Explain how the protein titin contributes to the passive and active viscoelastic properties of skeletal muscle, and how two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series can represent those properties across a range of muscle lengths and activation levels.
  3. Describe how active skeletal muscle stores and releases elastic potential energy during stretch, and explain why activation dramatically increases both the amount of energy stored and the time over which it is retained.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Kiisa Nishikawa

Overview

Introduction: From Frog Tongues to Muscle Mechanics

Dr. Nishikawa opened by noting that, while the workshop title refers to material properties in biomechanics, the content focuses specifically on material properties in muscle mechanics — a distinction she made after observing that standard searches on the topic emphasize bone, cartilage, and tendon while largely omitting muscle. Her interest in muscle material properties grew out of earlier research on how frogs and toads use projectile tongues to catch fast-moving prey. In these animals, the jaw-opening muscles reach their maximum length when the mouth is fully closed, so they cannot be actively pre-stretched. Despite this constraint, the muscles must store and release energy at very high rates. Understanding where that energy is stored in muscle — and how — forms the central question of the talk. The workshop proceeded from first principles (springs and dampers) through to current experimental work on tunable viscoelastic muscle mechanics.

Springs, Dampers, and the Foundations of Viscoelasticity

A linear spring obeys Hooke’s Law: the restoring force F equals the spring constant k multiplied by displacement x (F = kx). In normalized, material-independent form this is written as stress = elastic modulus times strain (σ = Eε). Stiffer materials have a higher spring constant and elastic modulus; more compliant materials have lower values. At small strains many materials behave in a Hookian (linear) way, but at high strain they yield and no longer return to their original shape. A damper (or dashpot) represents viscous resistance: the force it exerts is proportional not to displacement but to the rate of displacement (F = bẋ, where b is the damping constant). A corresponding normalized form relates stress to strain rate through a damping modulus. Both spring and damper properties scale consistently from the whole-muscle level down to the level of individual thick filaments and titin molecules within a sarcomere, because they depend on material composition rather than absolute size.

Viscoelastic Models: From Two to Four Elements

Combining springs and dampers produces viscoelastic models whose behavior depends on how the elements are arranged. The Maxwell model places a spring and a damper in series: stress is equal in both elements but strain is distributed unequally between them. Under a step increase in strain, the Maxwell model produces an initial large force that decays all the way to zero over time. The Kelvin-Voigt model places a spring and a damper in parallel: strain is equal in both elements and total stress equals the sum of the elastic and viscous contributions (σ = Eε + bε̇). Under a step increase in strain, the Kelvin-Voigt model shows a transient force increase that decays down to the residual elastic force in the spring rather than to zero. The Hill model places a spring in series with a Kelvin-Voigt element. More complex arrangements — two Maxwell elements in parallel, two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series, five or more element combinations — are also possible and each produces distinct force-strain behavior. The time constant of a Kelvin-Voigt element, τ, equals the damping modulus divided by the elastic modulus and governs how quickly force decays after a perturbation.

Measuring Viscoelastic Properties: Three Experimental Approaches

Three experimental protocols are commonly used to characterize viscoelastic properties in muscle. In a ramp stretch and hold, the muscle is stretched at a defined amplitude and velocity and then held at the new length; this approach mimics some in-vivo perturbations and allows efficient testing across multiple lengths and activation levels, but velocity transients at the start and end of the ramp mean the muscle is probed across a range of velocities, not just the nominal one, making model fitting complex. Sinusoidal perturbations oscillate muscle length at defined frequencies; elastic forces are in phase with the oscillation and viscous forces are 90 degrees out of phase, which is useful for decomposing the two contributions, but each activation level and starting length must be probed separately, increasing experimental time and fatigue risk. White noise perturbations vary muscle length randomly within a chosen frequency band, permitting multiple frequencies to be assessed simultaneously; they still require separate activation-level and starting-length conditions, but reduce total experiment time compared to sinusoidal methods. Kirsch, Boskov, and Rymer (1994; IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering) applied all three paradigms to cat soleus in a decerebrate preparation and compared how well a single Kelvin-Voigt element, a Hill model, and a standard viscoelastic solid (Maxwell element in parallel with a spring) fit the resulting data. No single model fit both the ramp-stretch and white-noise data sets: the Kelvin-Voigt element fit the white-noise data but not the ramp data at smaller amplitudes; the 3-parameter models fit ramp data but not white noise. The best-fitting model explained only 80–85% of force variance, which falls below the standard set by modern methods.

System Identification in Mouse and Frog Muscles

To identify a better viscoelastic model of muscle, Dr. Nishikawa and collaborators conducted ramp-stretch experiments across the full physiological range of muscle lengths in mouse soleus (ex vivo; stretch amplitude 5% L₀, velocity 1 L₀/s; starting lengths from 0.8 to 1.25 L₀ in 5% increments; n = 7–9 muscles per group) and in three bullfrog muscles (sartorius, iliofibularis, and semitendinosus). Three titin genotypes of mouse soleus were included: wild type, the naturally occurring Mdm spontaneous mutant (primary deletion of approximately 50 amino acids at the boundary of the N2A and PEVK regions of titin), and a transgenic mouse carrying a large deletion spanning exons 112 to 158 of PEVK titin — removing more than 1,500 amino acids, approximately 75% of the contour length of the passive PEVK spring. For each muscle, ramp stretches were performed both passively and at the plateau of isometric force. System identification showed that a model consisting of two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series fit all mouse and frog muscle data with high R-squared values and residuals on the order of 10⁻⁴. The only exception was the exon 112–158 transgenic mouse, whose unusual PEVK structure precluded a good fit — an exception that strengthens rather than undermines the model.

Titin Subdomain Assignment and Activation-Dependent Tuning

The two Kelvin-Voigt elements (k1/b1 and k2/b2) differ in both their passive behavior and their response to activation, and these differences point to distinct molecular origins within titin. The k1/b1 element, assigned to the PEVK1 and PEVK2 sub-domains of titin near the Z-disc, behaves as an exponential spring passively: its stiffness varies over approximately three orders of magnitude from the shortest to the longest muscle length tested, a hallmark of an entropic spring. Passive time constants for k1/b1 are approximately 100 milliseconds; upon activation they increase to roughly 0.5–1 second, meaning the active muscle can retain force stored in this element across a timescale comparable to a full locomotor cycle. The k2/b2 element, assigned to the PEVK3 sub-domain near the M-line, is less stiff and less damped than k1/b1 in passive muscle (time constants approximately 10 ms). Upon activation, k2/b2 stiffens substantially and transitions from an exponential spring to a linear spring; its time constants actually shorten further (to approximately 5 ms) in active muscle. The contrasting behavior of the two elements — k1/b1 becoming slower and k2/b2 becoming faster with activation — means that active muscle shifts energy storage predominantly into k1/b1 and can retain that energy much longer than passive muscle can. These assignments are corroborated by the pattern of differences between wild-type and Mdm muscles, by frog titin isoform data, and by published single-molecule atomic force microscopy data on titin PEVK sub-domains.

Power, Energy Storage, and Comparison to Prior Literature

Using the estimated stiffnesses from system identification, Dr. Nishikawa calculated the power and energy stored by the two Kelvin-Voigt elements during ramp stretches. Elastic energy stored = ½kx², which can also be written as ½F²/k, and approximately two-thirds of stored elastic energy dissipates within one time constant. For the frog sartorius, total power in the viscoelastic transmission rose from approximately 1 W/kg in passive muscle to approximately 100 W/kg upon activation; for the semitendinosus, from approximately 8 W/kg to approximately 200 W/kg — values that represent substantial fractions of the approximately 250 W/kg maximum power predicted by the force-velocity relationship. Energy stored in active sartorius reached approximately 0.063 P₀L₀ (where P₀ is maximum isometric force and L₀ is optimal length), compared to 0.0034 P₀L₀ passively. This active value is close to the 0.092 P₀L₀ total energy storage reported by Linari, Woledge, and Curtin (2003; Journal of Physiology) from single fibers of frog anterior tibialis — work that found only approximately one-third of stored energy explicable by cross bridges, thick filaments, thin filaments, and tendons combined. During Q&A, an on-the-fly unit conversion expressed these energy values as approximately 15–20 J/kg, which participants noted is non-trivial relative to the range of muscle work reported in the locomotion literature. Mdm muscles, which lack most of PEVK1/PEVK2, store approximately 10 times less energy in the active state than wild-type muscles — a result consistent with the k1/b1 element being the primary energy reservoir during activation.

Broader Implications and Q&A Discussion

In closing, Dr. Nishikawa characterized muscles as tunable, variable transmission-ratio viscoelastic actuators that can harvest energy from environmental loads during stretch and release stored energy to amplify or modify cross-bridge power output. Individual titin domains appear to have been shaped over hundreds of millions of years of evolution to provide specialized mechanical properties spanning orders of magnitude in frequency and force. The workshop concluded with extended Q&A covering several topics: the planned extension of the two-Kelvin-Voigt model to muscle-tendon units (adding a spring in series to represent the external tendon); the differences between fast and slow muscles (EDL vs. soleus share the same PEVK exon expression pattern, but the N2A actin-binding site of titin is located closer to the Z-disc in EDL, which Dr. Nishikawa and collaborator Rick Kessler described as the fast muscle “running in high gear”); the timescales over which stored energy is physiologically meaningful (energy retention lasting through a full gait cycle in the mouse and frog); and the development of open-source Lagrangian dynamics models intended to replace Hill models in simulations by incorporating cross-bridge activation dynamics, N2A actin-binding dynamics, thin-filament titin winding, and the length- and activation-dependent viscoelastic response characterized in this work.
