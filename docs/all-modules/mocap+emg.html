Using motion capture and electromyography (EMG) in movement sciences

Keywords

inverse kinematics, inverse dynamics, electromyography, motion capture, EMG signal processing

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, learners should be able to:

  1. Explain how inverse kinematics and inverse dynamics are used to compute joint angles and joint moments from motion capture data, and identify the assumptions underlying each approach.
  2. Describe how muscle action potentials are detected by bipolar electrodes and how surface EMG signals reflect the summed activity of multiple motor units.
  3. Identify common sources of error in motion capture and EMG data collection and describe practical strategies to minimize them at each stage of the processing pipeline.

Workshop

Presented by: Lauren Infantino, Kennedy Kerr, Autumn Routt, Emily Smith

Overview

Inverse Kinematics: Estimating Joint Angles

Inverse kinematics is a technique foundational to all motion capture software. It is used whenever joint angles need to be estimated from limited information — for example, at joint centers with no accessible bony landmark, such as the hip in humans, or at joints in animal models where placing markers directly is not feasible. Markerless motion capture systems use inverse kinematics internally to constrain their pose estimates to anatomically realistic configurations. The technique also applies when filling gaps in marker data: when a reflective marker is occluded during a trial, the software can estimate the missing joint angle from the angles of the remaining visible markers. The workshop worked through a hands-on example using the law of cosines to compute ankle and knee angles in a squatting figure, given known segment lengths and hip height. A key discussion point was that when taking the arc cosine to solve for a joint angle, the solution could be positive or negative; biological constraints typically allow only one of those answers (for example, a negative value would correspond to the knee bending backwards, which is not anatomically plausible in humans).

Inverse Dynamics: Computing Joint Moments

Inverse dynamics extends kinematic data to estimate joint moments by incorporating force information. Two complementary approaches were presented. The bottom-up approach starts from ground reaction forces measured by a force plate beneath the subject’s feet and uses the moment arm — the perpendicular distance from the force’s line of action to the joint center of interest — to compute moments, working upward through the body segment by segment. The top-down approach begins from a location where the external force is known or can be assumed: for example, no external force acts on the head, and a leg in swing phase during walking has no ground contact force. Starting from either of these locations and working toward the body’s center or lower extremities yields joint moment estimates by the same moment-arm logic. OpenSim uses both approaches simultaneously and compares their results; minimizing the difference between bottom-up and top-down estimates serves as an indicator of overall data quality. During Q&A, the group discussed using the right-hand rule (the cross product R × F, where R is the moment arm vector and F is the force vector) to formally determine the sign of a joint moment, and clarified that a free-body diagram can be cut at any joint provided all forces on the isolated segment are fully accounted for.

Best Practices for Motion Capture Data Collection

Motion capture is not a single measurement but a sequential pipeline of processing steps: markers are placed on the subject, cameras emit infrared light and detect the reflections from passive markers, software triangulates three-dimensional marker positions from multiple camera views, those trajectories are used to build a biomechanical model, and biomechanical variables are then computed. Error introduced at any stage accumulates as data moves through the pipeline. Markers are placed on bony landmarks to minimize soft tissue artifact — the displacement of a marker relative to the underlying bone when overlying skin or muscle tissue shifts during movement, which the software incorrectly interprets as bone movement. A single camera can only determine where a marker falls in its two-dimensional image; at least two calibrated cameras are required to triangulate a marker’s three-dimensional position. Markerless systems use computer vision algorithms, typically neural networks, to identify anatomical landmarks directly from video, which is particularly useful for small animals or behaviors where markers would be occluded or would interfere with the movement. A minimum inter-marker distance must be respected because markers placed too close together can switch trajectories between frames. Practical recommendations included using tight-fitting clothing, calibrating the lab system appropriately on each data collection day, monitoring marker trajectories throughout each trial with the help of an assistant, having participants cross their arms when they would otherwise occlude trunk markers, and re-collecting any questionable trial immediately rather than attempting to repair it during processing.

How EMG Works: Bipolar Electrodes and Signal Generation

Electromyography (EMG) captures information about the timing, magnitude, and duration of muscle activation. The signal originates in the spinal cord: alpha motor neuron cell bodies receive excitatory and inhibitory inputs at their dendrites, and when the summed input crosses threshold, an action potential travels along the motor neuron to the neuromuscular junction. At that junction, acetylcholine is released, which changes the membrane permeability of the muscle fiber and triggers a fiber-level action potential through sodium influx and subsequent calcium release, causing muscle contraction. A bipolar electrode detects the voltage difference between two electrode sites as this action potential propagates along the length of the muscle fiber. Surface EMG records the summed activity from many motor units across a volume of muscle, producing the characteristically noisy waveform familiar from published data. For bipolar electrodes to register a meaningful voltage difference, they must be oriented parallel — not perpendicular — to the muscle fiber direction. If the two electrodes are positioned such that they straddle the neuromuscular junction, the action potential reaches both electrodes simultaneously and the voltage difference is zero. In animal models, fine wire electrodes consist of a twisted pair of stainless steel wires that are insulated along their length with bare tips, inserted into the target muscle via a hypodermic needle.

Best Practices for EMG Data Collection

Reliable EMG recordings depend primarily on correct electrode placement. For human subjects, electrode placement begins with anatomical knowledge of the muscle’s origin, insertion, and function. Published electrode placement guidelines (referred to in the workshop as the “CDM guidelines”) provide recommended sensor placement procedures and anatomical reference information organized by muscle; presenters noted that these should be used as a starting point rather than followed rigidly, because the reference models tend to be able-bodied individuals and individual anatomy varies. Palpating for the target muscle while asking the participant to volitionally contract it — pressing firmly rather than lightly — is the most reliable way to confirm where the muscle lies before placing the electrode. Skin preparation (exfoliating with a product such as NuPrep, then wiping with alcohol) reduces impedance between electrode and skin. Electrodes and their associated hardware should be secured to prevent movement artifact, and placement should be verified by checking for clear activation bursts in the recording software before data collection begins. For animal models, the most critical preparatory step is testing the fine wire electrodes with a multimeter before insertion — checking for conductivity and confirming insulation is intact except at the bare tips — because stimulation-based verification of placement is generally not feasible. Keeping wire lengths short, securing wires to nearby tissue after insertion, and pressing down as the needle is withdrawn all reduce noise introduced by wire movement.

EMG Signal Processing: From Raw Signal to Envelope

The standard pipeline for processing surface EMG has three main steps. First, a high-pass filter (typically at approximately 20–30 Hz) is applied to the raw signal to remove the DC offset and any low-frequency motion artifacts. Second, full-wave rectification takes the absolute value of the filtered signal, converting all negative deflections to positive so the signal lies entirely on one side of zero. Third, a low-pass filter (typically at approximately 10–20 Hz) smooths the rectified signal into the EMG envelope — the representation most commonly shown in published literature and used for interpreting muscle activation patterns. Fast Fourier transformation (FFT) is a useful diagnostic tool before filter design: it converts the raw EMG signal from the time domain into the frequency domain, producing a power spectrum that shows which frequencies are present and at what magnitude. Inspecting this spectrum helps identify, for example, a 50 Hz spike from AC electrical sources (lights, power cables) that can be targeted with a notch filter. Butterworth filters are the most common implementation and come in low-pass, high-pass, bandpass, and notch variants; the cutoff frequency and filter order (which controls how sharply the filter attenuates frequencies beyond the cutoff) are the two main design parameters. Signal decomposition is a distinct analytical approach that uses electrode arrays and complex algorithms to separate the raw EMG into individual motor unit action potential trains, providing finer insight into motor control; it requires intramuscular or two-dimensional electrode arrays and is not feasible with standard bipolar surface electrodes. The workshop concluded with a MATLAB live script tutorial that stepped participants through the full processing pipeline using human tibialis anterior EMG data and rat walking data recorded from an animal model of Parkinson’s disease.
