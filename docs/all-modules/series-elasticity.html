Series elasticity

Keywords

series elasticity, tendon mechanics, muscle-tendon unit, power amplification, energy storage

Learning objectives

By the end of this workshop, learners should be able to:

  1. Describe the hierarchical collagen structure of tendon and explain how the crimped resting arrangement of collagen fibers gives rise to the nonlinear (toe region) stress-strain behavior that characterizes viscoelastic connective tissue.
  2. Distinguish among the three functional roles of in-series elastic elements during locomotion — power conservation, power amplification, and power attenuation — and identify the mechanical and biological conditions that favor each role.
  3. Explain how the mechanical matching of muscle force capacity and tendon stiffness influences energy storage during explosive movements, and how body size constrains the benefit of spring-driven versus direct muscle actuation.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Manny Azizi

What is in-series elasticity?

In-series elastic elements are structures that store and return mechanical energy when deformed, positioned in series with the muscles that produce force. Such structures appear across virtually the entire animal kingdom — invertebrates exploit resilient protein-based elements in modified exoskeleton structures, while vertebrates rely primarily on tendons and aponeuroses. Aponeuroses are broad, sheet-like connective tissues that allow muscle fibers to attach over a wider area than a long free tendon permits; they are loaded in more complex ways than free tendons but share the same fundamental elastic function.

Vertebrate tendons are composed predominantly of collagen arranged in a hierarchy of structural levels: tropocollagen molecules assemble into fibrils, fibrils bundle into fibers, and fibers organize into fascicles. Many fascicles constitute the whole tendon. Tendons are metabolically quiet tissues — tenocytes and fibroblasts maintain and repair the collagen matrix, but overall metabolic activity is low. This slow turnover has a practical consequence: tendon remodeling lags substantially behind muscle remodeling. Periods of rapid change in muscle properties (strength training, bed rest, injury recovery) can therefore produce temporary mismatches between the mechanical properties of the two tissues in series.

Tendon material properties: from nonlinearity to viscoelasticity

Stretching a tendon and plotting force against deformation reveals a characteristic nonlinear curve. At low strains the tissue is compliant — this toe region arises because collagen fibers sit in a crimped, wavy configuration at resting length. The initial stretch straightens those crimps before the fibers themselves bear substantial load. Beyond the toe region the relationship becomes roughly linear, and the slope — normalized by cross-sectional area and proportional length change — yields the elastic modulus, a size-independent material constant comparable across species and tissue sizes.

Tendons are not ideal elastic springs. When cycled repeatedly through length changes, they return most but not all of the stored energy. The energy lost per cycle is visible as the area enclosed by the loading and unloading curves (the hysteresis loop), and defines the resilience of the material. Resilience is high in locomotor tendons — studies of key energy-storing tendons report resilience values on the order of 90–93%, meaning very little energy is dissipated per cycle (Ker, Dimery, and Alexander, Journal of Zoology, 1986). The consistent energy loss with each cycle nonetheless confirms that tendon is a viscoelastic material rather than a true elastic spring. A second hallmark of viscoelasticity is stress relaxation: force decays when a tendon is stretched and held at constant length. Together, hysteresis and stress relaxation mean that effective tendon stiffness depends on loading rate, and that the full behavior of tendon cannot be captured by a simple spring model.

A framework for tendon function: three modes of operation

A useful conceptual framework maps tendon function onto the categories already applied to muscle function. Muscles can act as motors (producing energy), struts (producing force without net work), or brakes (absorbing energy). Tendons behave differently in each of these contexts, giving rise to three operational modes:

  • Power conservation — cyclic behaviors where energy from one stride is recycled into the next
  • Power amplification — explosive behaviors where tendon storage and rapid release boosts peak mechanical power beyond what muscle alone can deliver
  • Power attenuation — impact behaviors where the tendon buffers high-power inputs before they reach the muscle fibers

Each mode is explored in the sections that follow.

Power conservation during cyclic locomotion

In walking, running, and hopping, tendons allow muscles to minimize the mechanical work they perform. During ground contact, as the limb loads and the relevant joint flexes, mechanical energy from the center of mass transfers into the stretched tendon while the muscle generates force nearly isometrically — acting as a strut rather than a motor. In the second half of ground contact, as the joint extends, the tendon recoils and returns that stored energy to accelerate the body. Because the tendon handles the energy cycling, the muscle does little net mechanical work.

Classic experiments on tammar wallabies made this process unusually clear. In most running animals, metabolic cost increases approximately linearly with speed. In wallabies, once the animals transition from a quadrupedal running gait to a hopping gait, metabolic expenditure becomes largely independent of hopping speed — a finding established in kangaroos by Dawson and Taylor (Nature, 1973) and confirmed in tammar wallabies and red kangaroos by subsequent work (Biewener et al., J Exp Biol, 2004; Kram and Dawson, Comp Biochem Physiol B, 1998). Measurements of muscle force and fascicle length during hopping showed that the gastrocnemius and plantaris muscles generate large forces with very little length change — operating nearly isometrically — while the ankle joint cycles through large angles of flexion and extension (Biewener, Konieczynski, and Baudinette, J Exp Biol, 1998). Length change at the joint is absorbed by the tendon, not the muscle fibers. As hopping speed increases, the amount of elastic energy cycled through the tendon increases while muscle mechanical work stays approximately constant. The constancy of muscle work across a wide speed range directly accounts for the unusual metabolic independence of speed.

This raises a mechanistic question: why is it cheaper for the muscle to operate isometrically? One hypothesis holds that performing mechanical work (shortening against a load) is inherently more costly than generating force isometrically. Experiments measuring heat production from isolated muscles contracting under different conditions — including an isometric condition and a stretch-shorten cycle — found no significant difference in total energy expenditure between these conditions (Holt, Roberts, and Askew, J Exp Biol, 2014). This result supports a cost-of-force interpretation: the metabolic benefit of isometric operation comes not from avoiding mechanical work per se, but from avoiding the reduction in force capacity that accompanies shortening. When a muscle shortens, its instantaneous force capacity declines (the force-velocity relationship); to maintain a required support force, additional muscle volume must be recruited, and it is the recruited volume of muscle bearing load that drives metabolic cost. Tendons that allow muscles to remain near-isometric therefore reduce the total muscle volume that must be active for force support.

Power amplification: latch-mediated spring actuation

Explosive movements — jumping from rest, rapid acceleration, or climbing steep inclines — require large increases in center-of-mass mechanical energy over a short time. Muscles alone are limited in peak power output by the force-velocity trade-off: high forces require slow shortening, and vice versa. In-series elastic elements provide a mechanism to decouple the time over which muscle performs work from the time over which energy is delivered to the body.

A minimal model of this mechanism has four components: a motor (muscle), a spring (tendon), a latch, and a load (the body mass to be accelerated; Ilton and colleagues, Science, 2018). The motor slowly loads energy into the spring while the latch prevents premature movement of the load. When the latch releases, the spring unloads rapidly — over a shorter time than the motor’s contraction — delivering the stored energy at higher peak power than the motor could sustain. The bow-and-arrow is an everyday analog: the arm is the motor, the bow the spring, the fingers the latch, and the arrow the load.

The quality of the latch determines how efficiently stored energy reaches the load. Rapid, complete release is ideal; a slow or leaky latch dissipates energy during the unlatching phase itself. In vertebrate limbs, effective latching arises from joint geometry: at extreme flexion, mechanical advantage is low and the inertia of the body mass resists extension, holding the system loaded while the muscle builds force. As the limb extends and mechanical advantage increases, the effective latch releases. The timing of this release is sensitive to external conditions — for example, a compliant substrate extends contact time and modifies the effective latch, altering the energy delivery profile.

For a given muscle, there is an optimal tendon stiffness that maximizes stored energy. A spring that is too compliant allows the muscle to shorten so far along the force-velocity curve that force capacity falls before much strain energy accumulates. A spring that is too stiff allows too little shortening for meaningful energy storage. The optimal stiffness balances these limits, and comparative data suggest that muscle and tendon properties are co-tuned across species with different locomotor demands.

Importantly, spring actuation and direct muscle actuation are not mutually exclusive. Hybrid biological-computational experiments — in which isolated frog muscle-tendon units drove a simulated jumping model in real time — showed that during takeoff, the tendon is loaded and begins to recoil, but the muscle continues shortening and delivering mechanical power concurrently with tendon recoil. Contributions from tendon recoil and from ongoing muscle shortening were comparable during the unloading phase. This concurrent operation explains why frog jumping performance is sensitive to temperature even though purely elastic behaviors are temperature-insensitive: the temperature-dependent component is the muscle’s continuing power output during the unloading phase, not the tendon’s recoil per se.

Scaling: when do springs help?

The advantage of spring-driven actuation is not uniform across body sizes. Modeling of latch-mediated spring systems with muscle-like motor properties reveals a size-dependent crossover (Ilton et al., Science, 2018). For very small animals, spring-driven systems dramatically outperform direct muscle actuation across multiple performance metrics. As body mass increases, the inertia of the load increases; accelerating a larger mass takes longer regardless of how rapidly the spring recoils. This inertial constraint progressively erodes the power advantage of the spring, and above a transition body size the benefit diminishes substantially. Animals near this transition can potentially exploit both spring and muscle actuation simultaneously, as the frog experiments illustrate — and whether a given animal benefits more from elastic storage or direct muscle actuation depends on body mass, muscle properties, and the specific performance metric of interest.

Power attenuation: the tendon as a mechanical buffer

Landing from a jump, decelerating, or running downhill all require muscles to absorb and dissipate large amounts of mechanical energy rapidly. Eccentric (active lengthening) muscle contractions can accomplish this, but high-velocity or high-power input applied directly to muscle fibers carries injury risk. In-series tendons reduce this risk by acting as mechanical buffers between the environment and the muscle.

Two complementary mechanisms operate during impact loading. First, at the onset of loading, the activated muscle can shorten against the incoming force — even as the whole muscle-tendon unit is being lengthened — because the tendon absorbs the external deformation. This shortening moves the muscle fibers to shorter, safer positions on the force-length curve before any fascicle stretch occurs. Second, when the tendon subsequently reloads the muscle fibers (as stored energy dissipates and force decays), it does so at a lower velocity than the original external perturbation. The high-power input is temporarily stored in the tendon and released onto the muscle in a slower, more controlled manner.

In vivo recordings of turkeys dropped from height illustrate this buffering (Konow, Azizi, and Roberts, Proc R Soc B, 2012). At impact, as the ankle flexes rapidly, ankle extensor fascicles shorten rather than lengthen — energy enters the tendon, not the fibers. During the subsequent force-decay phase, the tendon recoils and stretches the fascicles, but at velocities substantially lower than the impact velocity. The mechanical power delivered to the whole muscle-tendon unit at impact substantially exceeds the power ultimately delivered to the muscle fascicles, demonstrating significant attenuation of the loading rate.

The timing of muscle activation modulates these protective effects. Anticipatory activation before impact — common in bipedal animals during the aerial phase of locomotion — produces more initial shortening, shifting operating lengths to safer positions before the eccentric phase begins. Activating the muscle earlier promotes greater shortening and moves fibers further up the ascending limb of the force-length curve; later activation reduces this protective shift. Experiments varying activation onset by tens of milliseconds relative to stretch onset showed that, while peak loading rates applied to the fascicles were similar across timing conditions, operating length — the position on the force-length curve during the stretch — differed substantially.

The rate at which the tendon reloads the muscle fibers depends on how quickly the muscle deactivates and force drops, allowing the tendon to recoil. Muscles with faster deactivation kinetics therefore experience faster tendon-mediated loading. This is consistent with the observation that fast-twitch fiber types — particularly fast oxidative glycolytic fibers — tend to show greater susceptibility to eccentric muscle damage than slow oxidative fibers under equivalent activation conditions. The proposed mechanism is that faster relaxation kinetics allow the tendon to recoil at a higher rate, imposing faster stretch velocities on the fibers. This remains a mechanistic hypothesis that warrants direct experimental testing.

An underappreciated consequence of in-series compliance is its effect on proprioception. When fascicles shorten while the whole muscle-tendon unit is being stretched, Ia afferents (muscle spindles) receive signals that do not accurately reflect the external mechanics — the muscle is shortening while the joint is flexing. This decoupling raises the possibility that Golgi tendon organ (Ib) signals, which monitor muscle-tendon force rather than length, carry more relevant information about the external mechanical state in systems with substantial in-series compliance. The implication is that in highly compliant tendon systems, reactive stretch-reflex responses are unlikely to govern the initial protective response; anticipatory, feed-forward motor commands are essential for appropriate pre-contact muscle conditioning.
