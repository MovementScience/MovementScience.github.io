System identification

Keywords

system identification, viscoelastic models, titin, transfer functions, muscle mechanics

Learning objectives

  1. Describe the difference between first-principles modeling and system identification, and explain when each approach is appropriate for studying biological tissues.
  2. Identify the components of simple viscoelastic models (spring, damper, Maxwell, Kelvin-Voigt) and predict how each element responds to a step stretch or a step force input.
  3. Explain how ramp stretch experiments and transfer functions are used to estimate the viscoelastic properties of muscle and tendon, and describe how those properties relate to energy storage, power amplification, and tissue structure.

Workshop

Overview

What Is System Identification?

Mathematical modeling in biomechanics follows two broad paradigms: first-principles modeling derives governing equations from fundamental physical laws with every parameter physically interpretable, while system identification builds mathematical models of dynamical systems from experimentally measured input-output data. System identification models serve three main purposes: characterizing and analyzing system behavior, predicting system outputs from a given input, and designing controllers that interact with the system. Models occupy a spectrum from white-box (high structural fidelity, parameters physically interpretable) through gray-box (known structure with data-driven parameter estimation) to black-box (minimal structural assumptions); research on muscle and tendon mechanics typically employs gray- and white-box approaches, where the model topology is informed by physical intuition and parameters are identified from experiment. The System Identification Toolbox in MATLAB, developed by Lennart Ljung, is the primary computational platform for these analyses; specialized code written by Rick Castler adapts these tools to ramp stretch and transfer-function analyses of muscle and tendon.

Mathematical Foundations: Laplace Transforms and Transfer Functions

The equations governing viscoelastic systems are ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in time; the Laplace transform converts these to algebraic equations by replacing time-derivative operations with multiplication by the complex variable s, greatly simplifying analysis. For a linear system with zero initial conditions, the ratio of output to input in the Laplace domain defines the transfer function — a rational polynomial in s whose numerator roots (zeros) and denominator roots (poles) together determine the system’s frequency response, transient behavior, and stability. Model selection among candidate topologies is guided by goodness-of-fit metrics (R², RMSE) and complexity-penalizing information criteria (Akaike Information Criterion, AIC; Bayesian Information Criterion, BIC), which guard against overfitting by penalizing unnecessary model complexity.

Viscoelastic Building Blocks: Springs, Dampers, and Their Combinations

A linear spring produces force proportional to displacement (F = kx) and is a static model, while a linear damper produces force proportional to velocity (F = Bẋ) and is a dynamic model. These elements combine into canonical two-element models: the Maxwell model (spring in series with a damper) cannot sustain a static force and behaves as a fluid, whereas the Kelvin-Voigt model (spring in parallel with a damper) sustains a static force and behaves as a solid with time constant τ = B/k. Across multiple species and preparations, skeletal muscle (cat soleus, frog sartorius and semitendinosus, rat medial gastrocnemius, and a range of mouse muscles) is consistently described by two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series, while tendon (rat tail) is described by a Kelvin-Voigt solid.

Experimental Identification of Viscoelastic Models

Three experimental protocols probe viscoelastic properties using length as input and force as output: ramp stretch and hold (transient force spike and subsequent relaxation), sinusoidal perturbation (elastic components respond in phase, viscous components out of phase), and white-noise pseudorandom perturbation (broadband frequency coverage). All three protocols yield equivalent parameter estimates in principle; ramp stretch is most similar to in vivo perturbations and allows systematic sampling across the physiological length range (approximately 0.8 to 1.2 times optimal length) with minimal fatigue. Parameters are estimated by minimizing the sum of squared error between the model response and experimental measurements using least-squares methods in MATLAB’s System Identification Toolbox. Passive muscles display a Kelvin-Voigt-like force response (spike followed by exponential decay), while active muscles produce a larger initial force and slower decay, with the second Kelvin-Voigt element (KV2) becoming substantially stiffer upon stimulation and concentrating nearly all elastic energy storage in the first element (KV1).

Energy Storage, Power Amplification, and Dissipation

Once spring constants k and damping coefficients B are identified, energy stored (E = ½kx²), power (P = F × v), and time constant (τ = B/k) follow analytically for each element. Because the two Kelvin-Voigt elements in the muscle model are in series and share the same force, the element with the lower spring constant stores more energy; upon activation, KV2 stiffens markedly and nearly all elastic energy concentrates in KV1, with KV2 functioning primarily as a mechanical brake. Linari and colleagues (2003) measured heat and work in single frog muscle fibers and found that cross-bridges, internal connective tissue, and filament compliance together could explain only a portion of total stored energy; active-muscle energy values obtained by system identification, expressed in the same P₀ · L₀ units, correspond closely to this previously unexplained fraction, suggesting that active titin viscoelasticity accounts for the remainder.

From Isolated Muscles to Whole-Animal Locomotion

In vivo recordings from guinea fowl running over unexpected obstacles show that during an unexpected step into a hole, muscle force is substantially lower than during a level stride despite a larger EMG signal — and the reverse occurs when stepping up — with both responses occurring faster than neural feedback can operate. These observations implicate the intrinsic mechanical properties of the muscle-tendon unit, particularly titin viscoelasticity, as an automatic stabilization mechanism. The viscoelastic framework also offers a mechanistic explanation for residual force enhancement — the sustained post-stretch force elevation beyond cross-bridge model predictions — as a property of titin rather than cross-bridge cycling.

Connecting Model Elements to Titin Biology

The two Kelvin-Voigt elements identified by system identification correspond to specific domains of titin, the giant elastic protein spanning the half-sarcomere from Z disc to M-line. The PEVK domain — a proline, glutamate, valine, and lysine-rich disordered segment — is the primary structural candidate: a transgenic mouse with a large PEVK deletion shows only a single Kelvin-Voigt element in series with a spring, and passive muscle spring constants change approximately 1,000-fold across the physiological length range (0.8 to 1.2 L₀), consistent with titin’s known exponential spring behavior. Isoform-specific N2 insertions (N2A in most skeletal muscles, N2B in cardiac titin, N2BA in some slow preparations) modulate effective spring length and stiffness, predicting and explaining isoform-dependent variation in viscoelastic parameters identified across frog (sartorius, iliofibularis, semitendinosus), rodent, and cat muscles.
