System identification

Keywords

system identification, viscoelastic models, titin, transfer functions, muscle mechanics

Learning objectives

  1. Describe the difference between first-principles modeling and system identification, and explain when each approach is appropriate for studying biological tissues.
  2. Identify the components of simple viscoelastic models (spring, damper, Maxwell, Kelvin-Voigt) and predict how each element responds to a step stretch or a step force input.
  3. Explain how ramp stretch experiments and transfer functions are used to estimate the viscoelastic properties of muscle and tendon, and describe how those properties relate to energy storage, power amplification, and tissue structure.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Kiisa Nishikawa & Dr. Adwait Mane

Overview

What Is System Identification?

Mathematical modeling in biomechanics follows two broad paradigms. The first derives governing equations from first principles — fundamental physical laws such as Newton’s laws of motion, thermodynamics, or electromagnetism — where every parameter has a known physical interpretation. The second is system identification: the process of building mathematical models of dynamical systems from experimentally measured input-output data. System identification models serve three main purposes: characterizing and analyzing system behavior, predicting system outputs from a given input, and designing controllers that interact with the system.

In practice, models occupy a spectrum. White-box models have high structural fidelity — the governing equations are well established and all parameters are physically interpretable; system identification is used primarily to estimate the unknown parameter values from data. Black-box models rely almost entirely on empirical input-output data with minimal structural assumptions. Gray-box models blend known structure with data-driven parameter estimation. Research on muscle and tendon mechanics typically employs gray- and white-box approaches, where the model topology (for example, a spring-damper network) is informed by physical intuition and the parameters are identified from experiment.

Some useful terminology: a dynamic system is one whose state evolves over time; a static system is at equilibrium. Constitutive models describe how a specific material responds to a physical stimulus (mechanical load, temperature, or electromagnetic field) and may be static or dynamic. Within Newtonian constitutive models, kinematic models describe the geometry of motion, quasi-static models neglect inertial forces relative to viscous and elastic forces, and dynamic models explicitly include inertial effects. Because “dynamic” is used in several senses across disciplines, it is best practice to specify “Newtonian dynamic model” when that is the intended meaning.

The System Identification Toolbox in MATLAB — developed by Lennart Ljung — is the primary computational platform for the analyses described in this workshop. Specialized code written by Rick Castler adapts these general tools to ramp stretch and transfer-function analyses of muscle and tendon.

Mathematical Foundations: Laplace Transforms and Transfer Functions

The equations governing viscoelastic systems are ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in time. A canonical example is the damped oscillator: a mass connected to a spring and a damper, driven by an applied force F. Applying Newton’s second law yields a second-order ODE relating position x(t), velocity ẋ(t), and acceleration ẍ(t) to F(t). Extracting the ratio of output to input directly from such an ODE is algebraically cumbersome because of the time-derivative terms.

The Laplace transform converts a time-domain function f(t) into a function of a complex variable s. In the Laplace domain, differentiation with respect to time becomes multiplication by s, converting the ODE into an algebraic equation. The inverse Laplace transform converts back; in practice, look-up tables of transform pairs handle most cases encountered in biomechanics.

For a linear system with zero initial conditions, the ratio of output to input in the Laplace domain is the transfer function — a rational polynomial in s. The roots of the numerator are the zeros of the transfer function; the roots of the denominator are the poles. Poles and zeros together determine the system’s frequency response, transient behavior, and stability.

A useful practical tool is the analogy between mechanical and electrical systems: for linear systems, velocity is analogous to voltage and force is analogous to current. A spring-damper network can therefore be represented as an equivalent electrical circuit, facilitating analysis and parameter estimation using established tools from electrical engineering.

Model selection — determining how many springs and dampers, and in what configuration — is guided by comparing candidate models using goodness-of-fit metrics (R², RMSE) and information criteria (Akaike Information Criterion, AIC; Bayesian Information Criterion, BIC). Information criteria penalize model complexity, guarding against overfitting: a more complex model will almost always fit data better, but that improvement may reflect noise rather than true system dynamics.

Viscoelastic Building Blocks: Springs, Dampers, and Their Combinations

The fundamental mechanical elements of viscoelastic models are the linear spring and the linear damper.

A linear spring produces a force proportional to displacement: F = kx, where k is the spring constant. This is a static model — it contains no time-varying terms. Normalizing by cross-sectional area (stress) and resting length (strain) yields the familiar stress-strain relationship, which is linear over a material’s elastic range.

A linear damper (analogous to a piston moving through a viscous fluid) produces a force proportional to velocity: F = Bẋ, where B is the damping coefficient. This is a dynamic model because the strain rate (first time-derivative of displacement) is time-varying.

These elements are combined into more complex models:

Maxwell model (spring in series with a damper): stress is equal in both elements; strain is distributed between them. Under a step stretch, force rises during the stretch and then decays to zero — because the damper eventually accommodates the applied displacement. This makes the Maxwell element a fluid: it cannot sustain a static force indefinitely.

Kelvin-Voigt model (spring in parallel with a damper): strain is equal in both elements; stress is distributed. Under a step stretch, an initial force spike from the viscous element decays with time constant τ = B/k, leaving a sustained elastic force. This element can sustain a static force and thus behaves as a solid.

More complex models are built by combining these two-element systems:

  • Maxwell solid: a spring in parallel with a Maxwell element (three elements total).
  • Kelvin-Voigt solid: a spring in series with a Kelvin-Voigt element; this structure is structurally similar to the Hill muscle model.
  • Two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series: the model consistently identified for skeletal muscle across multiple species and preparations.
  • Two or more elements in parallel or series: explored when simpler models fail to fit the data, with model selection guided by AIC/BIC.

Empirical findings from this laboratory’s system identification work indicate that skeletal muscles (cat soleus, frog sartorius and semitendinosus, rat medial gastrocnemius, and a range of mouse muscles) are consistently described by two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series, while tendons (rat tail) are described by a Kelvin-Voigt solid.

Experimental Identification of Viscoelastic Models

Three experimental protocols can be used to probe viscoelastic properties, with length as the input and force as the output:

  1. Ramp stretch and hold: the tissue is stretched at a controlled rate to a fixed length and held. The force response includes a transient spike and subsequent relaxation — characteristic of a Kelvin-Voigt-type element. The corners of the ramp introduce velocity and acceleration transients that effectively sample a range of frequencies, similar to the broadband excitation of white-noise protocols. This approach is most similar to in vivo perturbations (stretch from an antagonist muscle, ground reaction force, or an external mechanical perturbation) and allows systematic sampling across the full physiological length range (approximately 0.8 to 1.2 times optimal length) with minimal fatigue.

  2. Sinusoidal perturbation: a sinusoidal length input is applied at a given frequency; elastic components respond in phase with the input and viscous components respond out of phase, with the phase lag proportional to the element’s time constant.

  3. White noise (pseudorandom) perturbation: random length changes covering a defined amplitude and frequency bandwidth. In principle, all three protocols yield equivalent parameter estimates; they differ in practical advantages and ease of implementation.

Transfer functions for the tendon model (Kelvin-Voigt solid) and muscle model (two Kelvin-Voigt elements in series) are formulated as impedance transfer functions — the ratio of force to velocity in the Laplace domain. The tendon model transfer function has two zeros and one pole; the muscle model has two zeros and two poles. Parameters are estimated by minimizing the sum of squared error between the model response and experimental measurements, using the least-squares method implemented in MATLAB’s System Identification Toolbox.

Passive muscles display a Kelvin-Voigt-like force response (spike followed by exponential decay). Active muscles produce a larger initial force and a slower decay, indicating that activation changes the viscoelastic properties. Specifically, upon stimulation, the second Kelvin-Voigt element (KV2) becomes substantially stiffer, concentrating almost all of the elastic energy storage into the first element (KV1).

Energy Storage, Power Amplification, and Dissipation

Once spring constants k and damping coefficients B are identified, a full mechanical analysis follows analytically for each element:

  • Energy stored: E = ½kx² = ½F²/k
  • Power: P = kx²/Δt = F × v (force times velocity)
  • Time constant: τ = B/k; two-thirds of the viscous force dissipates within τ seconds

Because the two Kelvin-Voigt elements in the muscle model are in series, they share the same force. The element with the lower spring constant therefore stores more energy. In passive muscle, the two elements store energy in similar proportions. Upon activation, KV2 stiffens markedly, storing almost no energy; nearly all elastic energy in the active muscle is concentrated in KV1. This activation-dependent redistribution means one element functions primarily as a power amplifier and the other primarily as a mechanical brake.

These quantities scale with activation and muscle length in ways that connect to whole-animal mechanics. Linari and colleagues (2003) stretched single frog muscle fibers during activation and measured heat and work output using a thermopile, normalizing elastic energy storage to the product of maximum isometric force and optimal length (P₀ · L₀). Their analysis showed that cross-bridges account for only a small fraction of total stored energy, and that the combined contributions of internal connective tissue, thick and thin filament compliance, and passive titin could explain only a portion of the total — leaving a substantial fraction unaccounted for. Energy measurements from frog sartorius muscles using the system identification approach described here, expressed in the same P₀ · L₀ units, yield active-muscle values that correspond closely to this previously unexplained fraction. This convergence suggests that active titin viscoelasticity accounts for the missing energy.

Power amplification and dissipation are observed directly: upon activation of frog sartorius, total positive power increases by approximately two orders of magnitude compared to the passive condition, while KV2 simultaneously increases its dissipative output. These relationships bridge isolated-tissue mechanics to in vivo function.

From Isolated Muscles to Whole-Animal Locomotion

In vivo recordings from guinea fowl running over unexpected obstacles (Monica Daley, University of California, Irvine) illustrate the functional significance of muscle viscoelasticity. Simultaneous measurements of muscle fascicle length (sonomicrometry), tendon force (tendon buckle), and muscle activation (EMG) show that during an unexpected step down into a hole, the muscle force is substantially lower than during a level stride — despite a larger EMG signal. The reverse occurs when the bird steps up: force is amplified relative to the level-stride baseline. Both responses occur faster than neural feedback can operate, implicating the intrinsic mechanical properties of the muscle-tendon unit — particularly titin viscoelasticity — as an automatic stabilization mechanism.

These observations motivate the system identification approach: by characterizing viscoelastic parameters across the full physiological length range and activation state, the isolated-muscle model can predict the energy storage, dissipation, and power amplification that arise during locomotor perturbations. The viscoelastic transmission in the sarcomere may also explain residual force enhancement — the sustained force elevation that persists after active stretch beyond what cross-bridge models predict — as a property of titin rather than of cross-bridge cycling.

Connecting Model Elements to Titin Biology

The two Kelvin-Voigt elements identified by system identification are not purely phenomenological: converging evidence from transgenic and mutant mouse models links them to specific domains of the titin protein.

Titin is the giant elastic protein spanning the half-sarcomere from Z disc to M-line. Its I-band portion contains three functionally distinct elastic segments:

Tandem immunoglobulin (Ig) domains: a series of folded Ig-like domains that behave as an entropic spring, extending under low force with minimal force development. In the viscoelastic model, the tandem Ig region does not contribute substantially as a spring or damper element; it likely limits extension of the PEVK region without itself generating large forces.

PEVK domain: a proline (P), glutamate (E), valine (V), and lysine (K)-rich disordered segment that extends under force. The PEVK domain is the primary structural candidate for the two Kelvin-Voigt elements. Evidence: - A transgenic mouse with a large deletion in titin shows only a single Kelvin-Voigt element in series with a spring — consistent with partial deletion of the PEVK region collapsing two KV elements into one. - A mutant in which a portion of the PEVK region is shorter and stiffer shows a detectable shift in the identified viscoelastic parameters. - The spring constant of passive muscle changes approximately 1,000-fold across the physiological length range (0.8 to 1.2 L₀) — an exponential dependence consistent with titin’s known behavior as an exponential spring. - In slow postural muscles such as soleus, the PEVK domain contains more than 2,000 amino acids, conferring high compliance and late onset of passive tension development. Cardiac titin contains a substantially shorter PEVK domain, consistent with the high passive stiffness of cardiac muscle.

The PEVK domain appears mechanically non-uniform: one sub-domain is specialized for elastic energy storage and power amplification (KV1), while another provides dissipative function (KV2). This partition may reflect evolutionary optimization — one region tuned for energy efficiency during cyclic contractions, the other for damping and injury prevention during high-force eccentric loading.

N2 insertions: isoform-specific elements that modulate the effective spring length and stiffness of the titin molecule. In most skeletal muscles, the N2A element is positioned relatively close to the Z disc, producing a stiffer spring; in soleus, it is positioned farther from the Z disc, contributing to greater compliance. EDL muscles, which have a more proximal N2A position, show a larger increase in stiffness upon activation compared to soleus — consistent with the hypothesis that N2A position controls the sensitivity of titin-actin interactions to calcium. The N2B insertion (found in cardiac titin) adds approximately 40 nm to the tandem Ig region, moving the potential titin-actin interaction site closer to the M-line and increasing compliance relative to N2A skeletal isoforms. The N2BA isoform, present in some cardiac and slow skeletal preparations, contains both N2A and N2B insertions and represents the most compliant major isoform.

Together, these structural differences predict and explain the isoform-dependent variation in viscoelastic parameters identified across frog (sartorius, iliofibularis, semitendinosus), rodent, and cat muscles. System identification provides a quantitative framework for comparing these isoforms functionally and for testing mechanistic hypotheses about how titin architecture shapes muscle performance across species and physiological conditions.
