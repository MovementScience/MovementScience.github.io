Ultrasound imaging

Keywords

muscle fascicle tracking, ultrasound imaging, optical flow, force-length relationship, muscle architecture

Learning objectives

  1. Describe how ultrasound imaging works and explain how different tissue types — including muscle, tendon, bone, nerve, and blood vessels — can be distinguished from one another in an ultrasound image.
  2. Explain why tracking muscle fascicle length and velocity matters for understanding a muscle’s force-generating capacity and its role in whole-body movement efficiency.
  3. Identify the key steps involved in using fascicle tracking software to extract length measurements from ultrasound video data, and describe the trade-offs between optical flow and line detection approaches.

Workshop

Presented by: Dr. Luke Jessup

Overview

Why Measuring Muscle Fascicle Length and Velocity Matters

The force-generating capacity of a muscle depends on the instantaneous position on its force–length and force–velocity curves, both of which are determined by fascicle length and shortening velocity relative to the muscle’s optimum. Measuring these quantities during natural movement reveals how a muscle’s operating capacity changes from moment to moment, informing understanding of why particular movement strategies are adopted. Research combining dynamometry with ultrasound has shown, for example, that human soleus operates near the ascending portion of the force–length plateau during walking, and that the preferred walk-to-run transition speed corresponds to fascicle lengths and shortening velocities that favor force production.

How Ultrasound Works

An ultrasound transducer emits high-frequency acoustic waves and detects the echoes reflected from tissue interfaces; software converts the speed and strength of returning echoes into a two-dimensional image. The brightness of a structure in the image depends on its echogenicity: anechoic structures such as blood and fluid appear dark, while highly echogenic structures such as bone, tendon, and connective tissue appear bright white. Bone surfaces reflect so strongly that ultrasound cannot penetrate further, producing a bright line with an acoustic shadow beneath it; musculoskeletal imaging is performed in B-mode (brightness mode), while Doppler mode is reserved for blood-flow velocity measurements.

Identifying Tissue Structures in Ultrasound Images

Muscle is recognizable by characteristic striations produced by interspersed connective tissue and aponeuroses. Tendons are echogenic and circular in cross-section, tapering in diameter along their length, whereas nerves maintain a consistent diameter; the clinical mnemonic VAN (vein–artery–nerve) — in which a pulsing, anechoic vessel is found adjacent to a brighter circular structure — helps locate nerves in an image. A transverse probe orientation is generally best for differentiating between structures, while a longitudinal orientation is preferred for visualizing and tracking fascicles.

Ultrasound Probe Setup and Integration with Motion Capture

Standard handheld clinical probes cannot be secured to a limb during dynamic movement; flat-form-factor probes designed for musculoskeletal biomechanics research are strapped directly to the skin, often alongside a foam block of similar dimensions to prevent probe rotation, and secured with cohesive bandage. Synchronization with motion capture and force plates is achieved by connecting the ultrasound module to a shared analog signal box and issuing a common trigger signal to start and stop recording across all devices simultaneously. When using Echo Wave software, a dedicated sync plug-in must be enabled for the system to register external trigger signals correctly.

Fascicle Tracking Software: UltraTimTrack and UlTrack

UltraTimTrack (implemented in MATLAB) fuses two computer-vision methods — optical flow, which tracks pixel motion between frames and tolerates image noise but can accumulate drift, and line detection, which operates on each frame independently and is drift-resistant but noise-sensitive — with a tunable weighting parameter that balances the two; a built-in sensitivity analysis function allows users to assess how this parameter affects the resulting length trace. UlTrack (v5.3, also MATLAB-based) uses optical flow alone and is better suited to slow, controlled movements; it includes a keyframe-correction feature that allows the user to anchor fascicle length at defined points in the movement cycle to remove accumulated drift. Both tools allow user-defined regions of interest between the deep and superficial aponeuroses, and fascicle coordinates from the Simple Muscle Architecture plug-in for ImageJ can be imported into UlTrack to reduce subjectivity in fascicle selection.

Recommended Acquisition Settings and Limitations

A frame rate of approximately 150 Hz is a practical trade-off between temporal resolution and image quality, with most current systems reaching a maximum of approximately 210–220 frames per second; below approximately 100–120 Hz, automated tracking methods show markedly increased noise. Higher frame rates are obtained by reducing image depth, image width, and the number of ultrasonic beams per scan — settings that trade image quality for temporal resolution. A field of view of approximately 50 mm depth by 40 mm width is typical for lower-limb fascicle tracking applications.

Advantages, Limitations, and Practical Considerations

Relative to implanted sonomicrometry sensors, ultrasound is non-invasive, provides contextual information about surrounding tissue motion, and is portable and straightforward to synchronize with other data-acquisition systems. Its principal limitations are a maximum temporal resolution of approximately 200 Hz at best image quality and reliance on computer-vision tracking algorithms that can accumulate error during fast or highly perturbed movements. In field deployments, portable probes can be combined with markerless motion capture systems; wireless ultrasound transmission carries substantial bandwidth costs relative to wired systems, and universal timestamping provides a practical strategy for post-hoc synchronization when hardware triggering is unavailable.
